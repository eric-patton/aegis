# Procedural Worlds + Authored Narrative: Research for Aegis

## Overview

The core tension: procedural generation gives infinite, replayable worlds but tends to produce narrative mush (repetitive quest text, characters that feel like slot-machine outputs, no sense that anyone "meant" anything). Handcrafted narrative gives meaning and specificity but doesn't scale and gets stale on replay. Every game studied here solves this the same structural way: author a grammar, a set of reusable parts, and a set of rules for how those parts recombine, then let procedural systems drive the combination while a human-authored "spine" (fixed characters, fixed events, fixed locations) anchors the player's sense that the world is real and cares about them.

Five recurring layering techniques emerge across all the games below:

1. **Fixed anchor + procedural variation.** One or more elements of a system are hand-authored and constant across playthroughs (a starting village, a final boss, a "true" history), while everything generated around that anchor varies. Players calibrate their expectations against the fixed thing, then trust the varied things because they resemble it.
2. **History-as-content-generator.** Simulating or scripting a world's past (wars, dynasties, disasters) produces named entities, grudges, ruins, and relationships that quests and dialogue can reference. The history doesn't have to be simulated in real time: it can be generated once, up front, as a batch process, and then treated as fixed lore for that playthrough.
3. **Storylets / quality-based narrative.** Instead of a single branching tree, content is chopped into small independently-triggerable units gated by world/character state ("qualities"), so authors write once and the system decides ordering. This scales combinatorially without an explosion of hand-authored branches.
4. **Systemic memory.** Any system that remembers specific past events (who killed you, who you spared, who you insulted) and surfaces that memory back to the player later manufactures the feeling of an authored story out of pure bookkeeping.
5. **Grammar/replacement-rule generation for prose and events**, not raw randomness. Random noun/verb substitution reads as noise; a designed grammar with causal templates ("X was betrayed by Y, so X's descendants hate Y's people") reads as history.

The failure mode nearly all of them warn about is the same: when the player can see the underlying template (the "mad libs" problem), procedural content instantly reads as hollow, no matter how much variation exists on top of it. The fix is never "add more random variables," it's tighter coupling between generated content and something the player already cares about (their own actions, a specific character, a specific place).

## How notable games solved this

### Dwarf Fortress: history generation as a narrative substrate

Dwarf Fortress generates a full world history before play begins: civilizations rise, fight wars, found and lose settlements, and individual "historical figures" accumulate biographies of specific, queryable events (marriages, murders, artifact creation, becoming a werebeast, etc). This isn't decorative background, it becomes the actual content of Adventure Mode and colors Fortress Mode: a dwarf you meet may be the direct descendant of a hero from a war three hundred years prior, and the game can tell you that. Legends Mode exposes this history as a browsable, omniscient encyclopedia, which is itself a design lesson: procedurally generated history is more valuable if the player (or an in-fiction historian NPC/book) can query it, not just witness fragments of it.

The mechanic worth stealing is that individual generated "figures" persist and accumulate state across the whole simulated timeline, so a quest or artifact 10 hours into play can correctly reference an event from world-gen that the player never saw, and it feels like continuity rather than coincidence, because the game is tracking it as fact rather than flavor text.

Sources: [World generation - Dwarf Fortress Wiki](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/World_generation), [Advanced world generation - Dwarf Fortress Wiki](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/Advanced_world_generation), [Exploring Dwarf Fortress' Legends Mode](https://themadwelshman.com/exploring-dwarf-fortress-legends-mode/)

### Caves of Qud: static backbone, procedural wrapper

Qud's own developers describe their architecture directly: "The roguelikes that had the biggest impact on me were Omega and ADOM, both of which had static areas that were wrapped in procedural worlds" ([Game Developer interview](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/tapping-into-the-potential-of-procedural-generation-in-caves-of-qud)). Concretely:

- **Joppa**, the starting village, is entirely handcrafted: fixed architecture, fixed NPCs, fixed early quests. New players learn the game's tone and rules against something stable, which builds trust before the game hands them anything procedural. This is the "fixed anchor" pattern.
- **The Sultans**: six rulers whose reigns structure the game's deep history. Five are procedurally generated (names, personalities, notable events, causes of death) via what the designers call "replacement grammars" over roughly a dozen possible life events each; the sixth, Resheph, is fixed and identical in every world, giving players and community a stable reference point to compare their own generated history against. See the academic paper "Subverting historical cause and effect: generation of mythic biographies in Caves of Qud" (Grinblat & Bucklew), summarized via [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/319364267_Subverting_historical_cause_effect_generation_of_mythic_biographies_in_Caves_of_Qud) and covered in [GDC Vault: Procedurally Generating History in Caves of Qud](https://gdcvault.com/play/1024990/Procedurally-Generating-History-in-Caves).
- **Villages** are generated end-to-end from that history: each gets its own faction, architecture, relationship to local resources, oral storytelling tradition, proverb, signature dish, signature skill, named and unnamed NPCs, and quests linking back to the main quest line ([World generation, Caves of Qud Wiki](https://wiki.cavesofqud.com/wiki/World_generation)). The GDC 2019 talk "End-to-End Procedural Generation in Caves of Qud" (Grinblat & Bucklew) walks through the full pipeline (slides: [media.gdcvault.com PDF](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2019/presentations/Grinblat_Jason_End-to-End_Procedural_Generation.pdf)).
- **Relics**: procedurally generated legendary items are explicitly tied back into sultan lore (a relic might be "the weapon that won a decisive battle" or "a wedding gift"), so item flavor text is generated from the same history graph as quests, meaning an item you find can reference a person or event you've already heard about elsewhere ([Relic, Caves of Qud Wiki](https://wiki.cavesofqud.com/wiki/Relic)).
- The explicit design goal, in the developers' own words, was partly selfish: procedural history "meant we could play our own game without the experience being a mere review of hand-crafted content," which is a useful frame for a small team: generate what you'd otherwise get bored authoring by hand, and hand-author what needs to feel intentional.

### Wildermyth: storylets stitched by persistent characters, not plot

Wildermyth inverts the classic "string of pearls" model (rigid scripted "string" interrupted by freeform "pearls" of gameplay). Instead, the interactive story *is* the pearl and procedurally generated tactical combat is the connective string ([cjleo.com breakdown](https://cjleo.com/blog/the-power-of-wildermyths-modular-storytelling-in-game-design/)). Mechanically:

- A campaign is built from **modular story segments** ("events") that can be shuffled and swapped; only the opening and closing beats are locked to keep the overarching campaign coherent. Everything in between is drawn from a large pool and can, in principle, connect to any other module.
- Choices inside an event have two kinds of consequence: short-term (a buff/debuff carried into the next combat) and long-term (permanent changes to a specific character's stats, relationships, and even physical appearance, e.g. losing an arm and gaining a metal prosthetic that shows up in every subsequent comic panel).
- The GDC 2022 talk "Getting Players Emotionally Invested in Procedural Characters in Wildermyth" (Nate Austin) frames the whole design philosophy as: procedural generation applied to *character* rather than *plot*, resembling oral storytelling traditions where "the broad shape of a narrative can remain the same but details change" ([GDC Vault](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1027614/Independent-Games-Summit-Session-Getting), slides: [media.gdcvault.com PDF](https://media.gdcvault.com/GDC+2022/Speaker+Slides/GettingPlayersEmotionally_Austin_Nate.pdf)).

The key insight: because the *characters* persist and visibly accumulate scars, relationships, and legendary status across an entire campaign (and even into sequel campaigns as "legacy" heroes), players project narrative meaning onto random event draws. The continuity lives in the character sheet and the artwork, not in a hand-written plot.

### Shadow of Mordor / Shadow of War: the Nemesis system as narrative-from-bookkeeping

Monolith's designers explicitly framed the system around Self-Determination Theory: competence, autonomy, and relatedness ([Game Developer: Designing Shadow of Mordor's Nemesis System](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-i-shadow-of-mordor-i-s-nemesis-system)). The generative trick is almost entirely **memory, not generation**:

- Every orc captain is a lightweight bundle of randomized traits (strengths, weaknesses, personality quirks) but the *narrative* comes from the system recording what happens between that captain and the player: who killed whom, who fled, who got promoted because they killed you. The game then plays that history back to you the next time you meet them ("Remember me? You left me for dead").
- Player death is reframed as **content generation, not failure**: dying promotes the orc who killed you and gives them a scar/story, converting a loss state into new narrative material rather than a rollback.
- Designers described the framework as borrowed from sports narrative structure (draft, playoffs, championship): a scaffold of escalating stakes into which emergent events are slotted, rather than a scripted plot competing with the emergent one.

The lesson for a text-driven RPG: you don't need rich procedural generation to get an authored *feel*, you need a system that never forgets what the player did to a specific named entity, and reliably re-surfaces that memory in later dialogue/events.

### Failbetter Games / Fallen London / Emily Short: quality-based narrative (storylets)

Failbetter coined "quality-based narrative" (QBN) to describe Fallen London's structure, later formalized in the open StoryNexus engine. Emily Short's widely cited taxonomy, "Beyond Branching: Quality-Based, Salience-Based, and Waypoint Narrative Structures" ([emshort.blog](https://emshort.blog/2016/04/12/beyond-branching-quality-based-and-salience-based-narrative-structures/)), lays out three complementary approaches relevant to Aegis:

- **Quality-based narrative (storylets):** content is a short, self-contained unit ("storylet") gated by numeric "qualities" (inventory, skill levels, relationship scores, story-progress flags) rather than by position in a branching tree. A storylet becomes available when its prerequisites are met and, once played, changes qualities that unlock or lock other storylets. Because storylets are decoupled from each other, authors can add new ones without redesigning existing content, and because qualities are just numbers, procedural systems can set them (a generated "sultan died" event can flip a quality that unlocks a whole village's worth of grief-themed storylets).
- **Salience-based narrative:** rather than gating availability, the system scores every eligible piece of content against current world-state and picks the single most-applicable one (this is how reactive dialogue in games like Left 4 Dead and Firewatch works). This pattern is well suited to ambient dialogue/flavor text layered on top of a procedurally generated world: write many candidate lines tagged with conditions, let the engine pick the best-fit line for the current location/quest-state/NPC-mood.
- **Waypoint narrative:** conversation or plot is modeled as a graph of "topics" an NPC is trying to steer toward, with dialogue attached to *transitions* between topics rather than the topics themselves; the player can introduce off-path topics, and the system "heals" back toward its authored waypoints. Useful for procedurally-populated NPCs who still need to reliably deliver a handful of authored beats regardless of what the player says.

The unifying idea across all three: **decouple content from position in a single tree.** A tree's cost to extend grows exponentially with branch depth; storylets, salience pools, and waypoint graphs all grow closer to linearly, because new content only needs to know its own prerequisites/tags, not the entire history of paths that could lead to it. This is precisely the property a "no two playthroughs the same, infinite NG+" design needs, because content must be addable forever without a combinatorial rewrite.

Sam Kabo Ashwell's companion taxonomy, "Standard Patterns in Choice-Based Games," is the standard reference for branch-and-bottleneck, gauntlet, and other tree-shaped patterns QBN was invented to avoid; see the summary and links from Emily Short's blog category on the topic ([emshort.blog/category/quality-based-narrative](https://emshort.blog/category/quality-based-narrative/)).

### Ken Levine's "Narrative Legos": the grammar-first pitch (largely unrealized, instructive as a warning)

At GDC 2014, Ken Levine pitched breaking narrative into small, tightly authored, combinable units ("narrative legos") so a system could procedurally assemble scenes and dialogue rather than replaying the same five-year, twelve-hour scripted game every time ([Game Developer coverage](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/ken-levine-s-narrative-lego-s), [Sword & Source summary](https://swordandsource.ca/narrative-lego-a-gdc-talk-by-ken-levine/)). The idea never shipped as a full product. It's worth including because it names the aspiration directly (infinitely combinable, still-authored-feeling scenes) and because its non-shipping is itself the lesson: the hard part isn't imagining a grammar of story pieces, it's building the *combination rules* (what can follow what, and why it makes sense) which is exactly what storylet prerequisites and Qud's replacement grammars solve in narrower, shippable form. A grammar with no combination constraints is just randomness; the constraints are the actual design work.

### Skyrim's radiant quests: the canonical failure mode and why

Bethesda's own postmortem commentary is unusually candid about this. Lead designer Bruce Nesmith described "Radiant Story" as generating quests from components (location, enemy type, reward) where "some components are randomized but not all," triggered against roughly thirty defined player-action/world-reaction pairs ([GamesBeat interview](https://gamesbeat.com/bethesdas-nesmith-reflects-on-the-difficult-birth-of-skyrims-radiant-story-system/)). Concrete lessons from that piece and from player-facing analysis ([UESP: Skyrim:Radiant](https://en.uesp.net/wiki/Skyrim:Radiant)):

- **They tried applying it to the main quest and rejected it outright**: "Sounds great, would play horribly," because a hand-crafted throughline consistently beats a systemic one for anything the player is meant to experience as *the* story. Radiant systems were scoped deliberately to side content ("job quests" like fetch/kill/deliver), never to anything with named stakes.
- **Real-world logic does not equal good gameplay.** An NPC realistically demanding a stolen item back was cut because "it didn't feel good, you ended up always killing the guy": simulating realism can produce mechanically or emotionally bad outcomes that have to be pruned by hand, i.e., procedural systems still need an author pruning them for fun, not just for coherence.
- **Reused location pools break the illusion.** Players note that most radiant quests draw from "about a dozen possible locations" per hold, so the "infinite quest" is visibly a template running out of variety within a single playthrough, and the perceived variety collapses fast (source: [UESP Radiant page](https://en.uesp.net/wiki/Skyrim:Radiant) and general community critique summarized across the search results above).
- **No characters, no stakes.** The most common player complaint (echoed across UESP, Reddit-adjacent commentary, and mod descriptions like "Actually Challenging Radiant Quests" on Nexus) is that radiant quests have "no actual stories or characters to it," reducing play to marker-following. This is the clearest evidence that variation in *objective* (kill X at Y for reward Z) is not the same as variation in *narrative*: the player can feel a system cycling through slots even when the numbers behind those slots are technically infinite.
- Bethesda's stated fix, going forward from Skyrim, was to keep radiant systems narrow (ambient job-board filler, reactive NPC barks) and put authored effort where it's load-bearing: named quest-givers, unique dungeons, and the main and faction questlines remain fully handcrafted.

### Kenshi: emergent narrative with no quest system to speak of

Kenshi has no radiant quest system and barely any mandatory quests; instead it relies on systemic simulation (factions with goals, NPC squads with AI needs, injury/prosthetic/slavery mechanics that persist) so that stories emerge from the interaction of systems rather than being generated by a narrative engine at all ("a bunch of systems accidentally created drama, betrayal, and heartbreak," per community and press commentary summarized in the [Dinaburg Writes overview](https://dinaburgwrites.com/games-related/procedural-storytelling-in-game-design) and [Grokipedia's Kenshi page](https://grokipedia.com/page/Kenshi_(video_game))). This is a useful contrast case: Kenshi proves you can get "no two playthroughs the same" purely from mechanical systems plus persistence (permanent injuries, faction reputation, capture/slavery states) without any dedicated narrative-generation layer at all. The tradeoff is that Kenshi has almost no authored narrative arc; it works because the game doesn't promise one. Aegis explicitly wants authored-feeling quests and NPCs, so Kenshi's approach is a floor (systemic consequence and persistence are necessary) but not sufficient on its own.

## Pitfalls and failure modes (cross-game synthesis)

1. **The visible mad-lib.** The number one complaint about every weak procedural narrative system (radiant quests chief among them) is that the player can perceive the template. The fix is not more randomized variables, it's fewer, better-hidden seams: reuse the same generated facts (a name, a grudge, a relic) across multiple surfaces (dialogue, item flavor, quest text, ambient rumor) so the player encounters the same fact from different angles, which reads as a world that "knows things" rather than a machine filling blanks.
2. **Randomness without causal grammar reads as noise.** Qud's designers deliberately avoided a full historical simulation and instead used replacement grammars with causal templates layered after the fact ("subverting historical cause and effect"), because pure simulation is expensive and pure randomness is unconvincing; a designed grammar of plausible cause-effect templates is cheaper and reads as more intentional than either extreme.
3. **Applying procedural systems to the main spine kills investment.** Bethesda tried and explicitly rejected radiant-generating the main quest. Every successful hybrid game (Qud, Skyrim, Wildermyth) keeps its highest-stakes throughline handcrafted or heavily authored, and reserves generation for side content, ambient texture, and character-level variation.
4. **Realism is not the same goal as fun; simulate, then prune.** Bethesda's cut "demand your item back" NPC behavior is the model case: a system can be logically coherent and still be bad game design, so procedural rules need a human editorial pass, not just a consistency check.
5. **Storylet sprawl without curation overwhelms players and authors.** QBN critiques (echoed in Emily Short's writing) note that quality-based systems can produce too many simultaneously-available storylets, or become opaque to new authors until enough content exists to feel dense; some curation layer (recommending/surfacing what's "salient" right now, per the salience-based pattern) is needed once the storylet pool gets large.
6. **Systems without memory feel the same as systems with no story at all.** The Nemesis system shows that memory of specific past events, reliably surfaced back to the player, is often more important to "feeling authored" than the sophistication of the generation itself. A simpler generator with perfect memory beats a richer generator with none.
7. **Procedural history is wasted if the player can't query it.** Dwarf Fortress's Legends Mode and Qud's lore-callouts both depend on giving players (or an in-world proxy: books, bards, historians, gossip) a way to actually surface generated history contextually. Generated facts nobody ever sees are wasted authoring effort.

## Recommendations for Aegis

1. **Adopt a storylet architecture as the base narrative engine, not a dialogue tree.** Every quest, NPC interaction, and world event should be authored as a storylet: a text unit with numeric prerequisites (qualities like reputation, faction standing, generated-history flags, inventory, stats) and numeric effects. This is the only pattern in this research that scales additively rather than combinatorially, which is a hard requirement for infinite NG+ where content must keep being layered in without a rewrite. Build the quality/flag system early; it is the foundation everything else (procedural history hooks, radiant-style filler, faction reactivity) plugs into.

2. **Generate a history layer once per world (or NG+ cycle), not live, and make it queryable in-fiction.** Follow Dwarf Fortress and Caves of Qud: at world creation, run a batch history generator that produces named historical figures, wars, dynasties, ruins, and relics with causal grammar templates (not raw randomness), and store it as fixed lore for that playthrough. Then surface it through an in-world mechanism the player can actually query: books, bards, historians, gravestones, item flavor text. Reuse the same generated facts across multiple content surfaces (a quest, an item description, a rumor) so players encounter corroborating detail rather than a one-off random string, which is what makes generated history read as "real" instead of noise.

3. **Fix a small set of anchors per world and vary everything else around them.** Give every generated world at least one handcrafted or heavily-authored constant: a starting settlement (Qud's Joppa model), a small number of "true" named NPCs or a fixed antagonist archetype, and a fixed main-quest throughline shape even if its content is populated from generated history. Never let the radiant/procedural system touch the primary spine quest; reserve it for side content, filler quests, and ambient texture, exactly as Bethesda concluded after trying and rejecting a fully radiant main quest.

4. **Give the world a Nemesis-style memory layer independent of how "smart" the generation is.** Any NPC or faction the player meaningfully interacts with (spares, kills, betrays, aids) should carry a small persistent memory record that gets surfaced back in later dialogue/events, procedurally chosen from templates ("you're the one who..."). Since Aegis has no permadeath, this is a natural place to reuse Shadow of Mordor's core trick: player death/failure should feed the memory system (the bandit who beat you gets a title and a scar) rather than just resetting state, converting a loss into new content.

5. **Author character continuity, not plot continuity, as Wildermyth does.** Because Aegis has procedurally generated worlds and starting characters but wants real storylines, invest authoring effort in reusable *character-shaped* storylets (relationships, injuries, oaths, grudges) that attach to whichever NPCs and player character exist in this playthrough, rather than pre-writing plots tied to specific named characters that may not exist next time. Design the "string" (connective tissue: procedurally chosen encounters, travel, combat) to be cheap and generated, and put authoring budget into the "pearls" (character-defining choice moments) exactly as Wildermyth's inverted string-of-pearls model does.

6. **Treat radiant/filler quests as explicitly disposable ambiance, and say so through variety of surface rather than variety of objective.** For the inevitable "kill X at Y for reward Z" filler content Aegis will need to fill a procedural world, don't try to disguise the template (players always see through it, per every Skyrim critique found here); instead keep filler quests short, low-stakes, and clearly coded as ambient world-texture, and spend the "feels authored" budget on making the *text* around them vary via salience-based line selection (many tagged candidate lines competing for best fit against quest-giver personality, location, and current world history) so at least the flavor changes even when the mechanical shape repeats.
