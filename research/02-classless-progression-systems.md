# Classless Character Progression: Stats, Skills, and Equipment as Build Identity

## Overview

Aegis has no character classes: everything a character can do has to fall out of three interacting systems, stats, skills, and equipment. This is a well trodden but genuinely hard design space. The core tension in every game surveyed here is the same one Aegis will face:

1. **Use based ("learn by doing") progression** feels organic and diegetic (you get better at archery by shooting arrows) but is trivially exploitable by repetitive, low-risk, low-cost actions unless the designer actively closes the loopholes.
2. **Point buy / level up allocation** (spend points on a level up) is easy to balance and hard to exploit, but breaks immersion (why did picking a lock make my character smarter?) and invites "efficient leveling" spreadsheet-math play that fights the roleplay fantasy.
3. **Equipment gating via stat/skill requirements** is what makes stats matter moment to moment (not just in menus) and is the main lever classless games use to create build identity without ever naming a "class."

Below: concrete mechanics from Kingdom Come: Deliverance, The Elder Scrolls (Morrowind through Skyrim, including the Oblivion leveling debacle), Dark Souls/Elden Ring, Caves of Qud, Cataclysm: Dark Days Ahead, and Path of Exile, followed by recommendations for Aegis.

## Quick comparison

| Game | Advancement model | Build identity lever | Respec | Main failure mode observed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kingdom Come: Deliverance | Pure use-based skills, perks at thresholds | Perk choices at thresholds, gear proficiency | None (by design) | Slow early grind; some low-risk loops once affordable |
| Morrowind | Use-based skills, attribute gain per level tied to skills raised | Attribute/skill combination, class as flavor only | None | "Efficient leveling" spreadsheet play |
| Oblivion | Use-based skills, level-up triggered by major skills only | Class choice, attribute min-max | None (until 2025 Remaster fix) | Playing in-character actively hurt you |
| Skyrim | Use-based skills, perk trees, attributes mostly removed | Perk tree shape per skill | Legendary skill reset (exploitable) | Fortify Restoration loop; infinite Legendary grinding |
| Dark Souls / Elden Ring | Point-buy attributes at level-up, spent on death-currency | Soft-capped stat curve, gear stat requirements | Rare, consumable-gated (Larval Tear) | None major; some player friction over scarcity |
| Caves of Qud | Point-buy skills, drafted/rolled mutations | Stat and mutation combination, physical/mental independence | Limited | N/A, mutation randomness itself is the design |
| Cataclysm: DDA | Use-based skills plus discrete proficiencies | Skill plus proficiency combination | Not applicable (roguelike) | "Stats through skills" reading exploit |
| Path of Exile | Point-buy shared passive web | Tree pathing and Keystones | Costly, currency-gated (Orb of Regret) | "Illusion of choice," requires external tools to navigate |

## How notable games solved this

### Kingdom Come: Deliverance: use-based skills, gated by risk and NPC training

KCD has no classes. Combat and non-combat skills (Strength, Agility, Vitality on one axis; Reading, Alchemy, Herbalism, Speech, etc. on the other) level purely through use: swing a sword, your Warfare/weapon skill ticks up; pick locks, Thievery ticks up; talk your way out of trouble, Speech ticks up. Perks unlock at skill thresholds and are chosen by the player, giving a lightweight "build" layer on top of pure use-based advancement. (https://kingdom-come-deliverance.fandom.com/wiki/Skills, https://kingdom-come-deliverance.fandom.com/wiki/Levelling_skills_in_the_early_game)

KCD's mitigations against degenerate grinding, several of which are instructive:

- **Some skills cannot be raw-grinded at all.** Reading and Pickpocketing explicitly require an initial unlock/training step before use-based gains even apply, closing the "repeat the free action a thousand times from level 1" loop for the skills most prone to it.
- **Diegetic cost is attached to the repeated action.** Most skill-raising actions cost real in-fiction resources (arrows, lockpicks that break, herbs, stamina, time of day, NPC patience/suspicion), so grinding has an opportunity cost instead of being free. This is the single biggest difference from Skyrim's alchemy/enchanting loop below, where the "cost" of the action was reduced to zero by the same exploit that inflated the reward.
- **Progression is capped by attribute/practice realism, not by a hard skill cap.** The game leans on friction (durability loss, guard reactions, needing sleep/food) rather than an arbitrary "you can only train this 5 times per day" counter, which keeps it feeling like a simulation rather than a meter.

The tradeoff: KCD's skill grind is still perceptibly a grind in the early game (the wiki page above is literally a guide to "leveling skills in the early game" because early Henry is so weak), and players do document repeatable, low-risk loops (e.g., repeatedly brewing cheap potions, repeatedly picking the same practice locks) once the friction is affordable. KCD accepts a mild version of the problem rather than solving it perfectly; it wins mostly by making the exploitable actions still cost something (ingredients, time, tool durability) so grinding is slow enough not to trivialize the game.

### The Elder Scrolls: the canonical case study in use-based failure modes, and how the series patched itself

The Elder Scrolls line is the most-cited cautionary tale for use-based skills, and it went through three distinct eras worth studying:

**Morrowind (2002):** 27 skills, 8 governing attributes, classes exist but are largely a starting-skill-weighting flavor, not a hard gate. Attribute increases were awarded per level-up based on which skills you'd raised, encouraging genuine specialization; "no one character could do everything," producing highly personalized runs (https://gamerant.com/elder-scrolls-morrowing-versus-skyrim-things-better/). But this is also the origin of the min-max "efficient leveling" doctrine that plagues the whole series: because attribute gain on level-up was determined by *how many* skills under each attribute you'd trained (capped per level), the optimal play was to carefully engineer exactly 10 skill-ups per level split 3/3/4 across your target attributes, i.e., to stop using skills you "shouldn't" once you'd hit the cap for that level. That is a purely metagame behavior with zero relationship to roleplay.

**Oblivion (2006)** inherited and worsened this: level-ups were triggered by raising *major* skills (the ones tied to your class fantasy), and if you played "in character" (a thief sneaking and picking locks) you leveled too fast for too few attribute points, while the world's aggressive level-scaling meant enemies got stronger in lockstep. The documented "fix" was to deliberately avoid using your class's own skills and instead grind unrelated minor skills (heavy armor, unused spells) purely to control the pace and shape of attribute gain, i.e., optimal play required *not playing your character* (https://en.uesp.net/wiki/Oblivion:Efficient_Leveling, https://www.pcgamer.com/games/rpg/praise-the-nine-oblivion-remastered-doesnt-make-you-grind-your-minor-skills-to-level-efficiently-and-increasing-endurance-boosts-your-hit-points-retroactively/). This is the paradigm case of a use-based system whose *meta-layer* (level-up attribute math) fights the *base-layer* (use skills you enjoy). Nineteen years later, the 2025 Oblivion Remastered patched exactly this: both major and minor skills now count toward leveling, and you get a flat number of attribute points per level regardless of which skills you raised, eliminating the need to game the system (same PC Gamer source above).

**Skyrim (2011)** cut attributes almost entirely (down to three derived pools: health/magicka/stamina) and replaced the attribute/class layer with per-skill perk trees, explicitly to remove the min-max incentive and widen accessibility (https://gamerant.com/the-elder-scrolls-classic-features-mechanics-cut-in-the-sequels/). This closed the Oblivion-style meta-exploit but reopened the raw grinding problem at the base layer, and famously worse: the **fortify-restoration loop** let players craft an alchemy-boosting enchantment, brew a Fortify Restoration potion, drink it, then re-equip the now-under-priced gear to make the *next* potion effectively free and absurdly powerful, because skill-experience-from-value created a feedback loop between two crafting skills that was never capped (https://www.neoseeker.com/the-elder-scrolls-v-skyrim/faqs/249803-skyrim-alchemy-exploit.html). Separately, the **Legendary Skill** feature, reset a skill to 15 and refund its perk points once it hits 100, was explicitly added as a "soft" level cap, but because it can be repeated indefinitely on the single cheapest skill to raise (classically: casting a 0-cost spell, or crafting trivial potions/enchants), it turns the level cap into an *infinite* grind rather than a real ceiling (see Quora/GameFAQs summaries above). The lesson: removing the point-buy meta-game does not remove the underlying incentive to find the cheapest repeatable action; it just relocates the exploit from "attribute math" to "the single degenerate loop the tuning missed."

**The general pattern across all three games:** any use-based system that ties advancement to a *numeric proxy* for effort (potion gold value, spell cast count, lockpick attempt count) rather than to genuinely scarce resources or genuinely risky context will eventually be gamed, because players (and especially min-maxers) will find the proxy's cheapest possible input. KCD survives longer than Skyrim specifically because its proxies (durability, ingredients, NPC reactions, sleep/food) are harder to zero out than "cast this free spell in a loop."

### Elden Ring / Dark Souls: attributes plus equipment requirements as the whole "class" system

FromSoft's souls games have no classes either, only starting "Classes" that are pure stat/gear presets you can immediately diverge from. Build identity comes from two interacting levers:

1. **Soft caps on damage-scaling attributes.** Points before a threshold give close to full value; points after give sharply reduced value. In Elden Ring, Vigor's first soft cap sits around 40 (roughly 1450 HP), with a second, weaker one near 60; Mind softcaps near 60; Endurance softcaps near 50 for stamina and 60 for equip load; the damage stats (Strength/Dexterity/Intelligence/Faith/Arcane) have multiple softcaps around 16 to 20, and again near 50 to 60, then again near 80 for the most committed single-stat builds (https://gamerant.com/elden-ring-stat-attribute-soft-hard-caps-diminishing-returns/, https://game8.co/games/Elden-Ring/archives/461478). Dark Souls 1/3 use the same pattern: Strength and Dexterity both softcap around 20 and again around 40, with an extra Dexterity-specific softcap near 45 tied to cast speed (https://darksouls.wiki.fextralife.com/Strength). This produces natural "build archetypes" (pure Strength, pure Dex, a 40/40 "Quality" hybrid) purely from the shape of the curve, with no class label attached to any of them.
2. **Hard stat *requirements* on gear**, separate from scaling. A weapon might require 26 Strength just to wield two-handed at all, regardless of how much damage it deals once wielded; under-meeting the requirement imposes a stamina/damage penalty rather than an outright block, which is a softer gate than a strict lockout. This is the mechanism that makes stat allocation *legible in the equipment screen*, a player can look at a weapon and immediately understand "I am not built for this yet," which is exactly the kind of readable gating a stat-only, class-free system needs.
3. **Deliberately scarce respec.** Elden Ring gates full stat reallocation behind Larval Tears, a rare, capped-per-playthrough consumable (about 18 per base-game run, carrying over into NG+), obtained only after defeating a specific boss (https://eldenring.wiki.fextralife.com/Larval+Tear). The explicit design intent is to make stat allocation a real, weighty decision rather than a free toggle, while still allowing course-correction for players who commit to a build that turns out to feel bad or gets outclassed by a cooler weapon found later. Community sentiment is mixed, some players want more given how many viable weapons/builds exist to sample, but the friction is clearly intentional rather than an oversight.

### Caves of Qud and Cataclysm: DDA: fully classless roguelikes

**Caves of Qud** has no classes at all: a character is defined by attributes (Strength, Agility, Toughness, Intelligence, Willpower, Ego), a skill tree bought with points, and, for Mutated Humans, a pool of randomly-offered or player-chosen mutations (physical like Multiple Arms/Carapace, mental like Teleportation/Precognition) that are mechanically independent of skills (https://wiki.cavesofqud.com/wiki/Mutations). Build identity comes from the *combination* of a stat spread, a skill selection, and a mutation set, none of which requires or implies the others, a true classless design where "wizard" and "warrior" are emergent labels players apply after the fact, not systems the game encodes. The interesting design point for Aegis: Qud explicitly keeps physical power (stats, skills, physical mutations) and "caster" power (mental mutations) on separate axes so they compose freely rather than needing to be traded off against each other, avoiding an implicit class dichotomy re-emerging from the math.

**Cataclysm: Dark Days Ahead** separates three progression axes: attributes (Strength/Dexterity/Intelligence/Perception), broad skills (Fabrication, Survival, Melee, etc.) that raise through use and through reading, and narrow **proficiencies** that gate specific recipes/actions independent of skill level (e.g., knowing "wood construction" as a discrete fact you either have or don't, regardless of your Fabrication number) (https://docs.cataclysmdda.org/JSON/PROFICIENCY.html). This is a deliberate answer to the "one number can't represent everything you know" problem: a broad skill measures general competence while a proficiency measures a specific, sometimes binary, unlockable piece of know-how, which is a useful pattern for representing recipes/lore/factional knowledge in Aegis without inventing 200 micro-skills. CDDA also shipped an optional "Stats Through Skills" mod/mode where raising skills nudges attributes, and its own community immediately found the exploit: reading books grants stat increases regardless of how high the stat already is, so a build can stack physical stats purely through passive reading with no in-fiction justification (a mage reading a repair manual gaining Strength), which the community discussion explicitly frames as needing a cap or a "must come from actual practice, not passive study" fix (https://discourse.cataclysmdda.org/t/stats-through-skills-is-amazing-and-broken/20614). This is the same failure family as Skyrim's alchemy loop: a progression path (reading) that was cheaper/safer than the "intended" path (practice) was granting the same reward.

Separately, CDDA's own team has an open issue thread about "slowing the rate of skill gain," and their design docs acknowledge that many starting professions "have practically no gameplay impact" beyond a skill point or two, i.e., even in a fully classless simulation-heavy roguelike, the temptation to reintroduce lightweight class-like presets (professions) as flavor/convenience keeps recurring, they just carry zero mechanical exclusivity.

### Path of Exile: the skill web, and why it may not fit Aegis

PoE's passive tree is the extreme end of "classless via massive shared graph": roughly 1300+ nodes shared by all characters, who merely start at different points on the wheel aligned to a starting class flavor, with "Notable" nodes acting as landmarks and rare "Keystone" nodes that flip fundamental rules (https://www.pathofexile.com/passive-skill-tree). It genuinely produces enormous build diversity and a strong sense of ownership over a build's shape. But the community's own long-running complaints (multiple forum threads literally titled "The Illusion of Choice") point at real costs: the tree is large enough that near-optimal pathing is effectively solved by external tools (Path of Building) before a single point is spent in-game, most of the graph functions as a tax you pay to reach a small number of build-defining Notables/Keystones rather than as itself being a meaningful choice, and correcting a mistake costs a scarce, purchased currency (Orbs of Regret) at a scale (tens of orbs for a 20-node correction) that punishes exploration rather than rewarding it. For a single-player, TUI, no-multiplayer-economy game like Aegis, the two things that make PoE's tree work, a min-max community pre-solving the graph, and a player-driven currency market that prices respec, are exactly the things Aegis will not have. Importing PoE-scale graph complexity without importing its ecosystem risks giving players an overwhelming, spreadsheet-shaped chore with none of the compensating infrastructure (build guides, planners, an economy to pay for mistakes). This argues for something with PoE's *spirit* (a shared, classless allocation space) at a small fraction of its *scale*.

## Pitfalls and failure modes, summarized

1. **Free-input grinding loops.** Any use-based gain tied to a repeatable action with near-zero cost (jumping in place, casting a 0-cost spell, brewing a trivial potion in bulk) will be found and abused. Fix by attaching real, non-trivial cost (consumable resources, durability, time-of-day/fatigue, risk of failure/detection) to every action that grants skill.
2. **Metagame-optimal play diverging from roleplay-optimal play.** Oblivion's efficient-leveling doctrine required deliberately not using your character's defining skills. Any system where the *rate* or *shape* of advancement is a separate, gameable resource from the *use* that ostensibly drives it will produce this. Prefer systems where the skill you use is the skill that grows, full stop, with no intervening attribute-point-per-level abstraction to game.
3. **Feedback loops between two systems that both scale off each other.** Skyrim's alchemy/enchanting loop is the classic version: skill experience scaled off value, and value could be inflated by the same skill's own tools. Any circular scaling relationship (X boosts Y, Y is used to measure progress in X) needs an explicit cap or a one-directional design.
4. **"Soft" level caps that are actually infinite.** Skyrim's Legendary Skill reset was meant to be a soft cap but is trivially chainable on the single cheapest skill, becoming an unlimited grind. A cap mechanism must not be repeatable on a fixed, degenerate input; if it must be repeatable, the *cost* of repeating it must scale up each time.
5. **Point-buy without stakes reads as a spreadsheet, not a character.** Systems that let you freely reallocate everything at will (no respec cost) remove the tension that makes commitment meaningful; systems that never let you reallocate at all (KCD skills, mostly) punish early-game ignorance forever. FromSoft's answer, rare but not zero respec, is a reasonable middle point.
6. **Overly large shared allocation graphs need supporting infrastructure Aegis will not have.** PoE's tree works because of community tooling and an economy; without those, equivalent scale becomes drudgery rather than expression.
7. **Professions/presets sneaking back in as "classes" through the side door.** CDDA and Oblivion both show that even nominally classless games keep reintroducing lightweight starting-package flavor (professions, birthsigns/classes as stat/skill weightings) because players want a fast, legible starting identity. This is fine as long as it never becomes a hard mechanical gate.

## Recommendations for Aegis

1. **Use-based skills with hard-costed practice, not free repetition.** Every action that raises a skill should consume something non-trivial and non-instantly-renewable in the fiction: a durable tool that wears, a limited daily window (fatigue/sleep-gated, similar to KCD), a consumable ingredient, or exposure to real risk/consequence (a failed lockpick attempt alerts guards; a botched alchemy batch wastes rare herbs and can poison you). Never let skill experience be a function of a value/price number that the same skill's tools can inflate; if brewing potions grants XP, gate it on quantity of distinct recipes attempted or on real-world scarcity of ingredients, not on gold value, closing the Skyrim alchemy loop by construction.
2. **No attribute-point-per-level abstraction between "use" and "growth."** Skip the Morrowind/Oblivion pattern entirely: don't let the *choice of which skills to raise* also secretly control a separate attribute-point budget that rewards not using your character's actual playstyle. If Aegis has core attributes (Strength, Agility, etc.), let them rise directly and slowly from sustained, heavy use of the skills that depend on them (a Cataclysm-style "stats through skills," but capped hard per attribute, e.g., +1 max per in-game year or per milestone, and only from actual use, never from passive reading/study) rather than from a level-up ceremony players can game.
3. **Soft caps on every scaling stat, hard requirements on gear.** Adopt the Souls/Elden Ring model directly: diminishing (not zero) returns on stats past a threshold (encourages breadth without banning depth), plus explicit stat/skill *requirements* on weapons and armor that are visible in the item's description before the player invests (so gear itself teaches the player what a "build" looks like, without ever naming a class). This is the single highest-leverage lever for build identity in a classless game because it is legible at the moment of choosing gear, not buried in a menu.
4. **Separate "broad skill" from "narrow proficiency/perk," CDDA-style.** Use a small number of broad skills (a dozen or so: One-Handed, Bows, Alchemy, Persuasion, Stealth, etc.) that rise smoothly with use and drive numeric competence, plus a much smaller number of discrete, unlockable perks or proficiencies gained at skill thresholds or from specific NPCs/books/quests (KCD's perk-at-threshold pattern) that grant qualitative new capabilities (a lockpicking technique, a fencing riposte, a recipe). This gives "spec" flavor and moment-to-moment choice without needing a PoE-scale graph, and keeps the UI legible in a terminal/TUI.
5. **Bounded, costly respec, never free, never permanent-lockout.** Give players a rare, discoverable, lore-justified respec resource (a Larval-Tear equivalent, perhaps an NPC "trainer" or a rare consumable found a handful of times per playthrough, refreshed on NG+ entry since the world regenerates harder anyway) rather than either unlimited free reallocation or KCD's total absence of respec. This preserves the weight of early choices while giving an escape valve for players who followed a bad build blindly for the first several hours, which matters even more in Aegis given permadeath is explicitly off the table but bad-build frustration is still a real churn risk.
6. **Keep the shared allocation space small and let equipment do the heavy lifting for variety.** Do not build a 1000+ node PoE-style web; Aegis has no player economy or community tooling to make that scale healthy. Instead, get most of the "endless build variety" pillar from the combinatorics of (stats x skills x perks x equipment affixes/procedural gear), which is already combinatorially huge, and keep the pure allocation graph (skills/perks) compact enough to reason about in a text UI without external planners.
