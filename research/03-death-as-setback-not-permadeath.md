# Death as a Setback, Not a Permadeath: Research for Aegis

## Overview

Aegis needs death to feel like a real cost, something the player genuinely wants to avoid, without ever deleting the hours they invested in a character. This is a well-trodden design space with decades of iteration across MMOs, action-RPGs, and roguelites, and the field has largely converged on a few insights:

1. Loss aversion (people feel losses roughly twice as intensely as equivalent gains) is the psychological engine behind every death penalty. A good penalty leans on this without breaking the player's motivation to keep playing ([Wolfshead: The Death Penalty Mechanic and Loss Aversion in MMO Design](https://wolfsheadonline.com/the-death-penalty-mechanic-and-loss-aversion-in-mmo-design/); [Psychology of Games podcast on loss aversion](https://www.psychologyofgames.com/2020/09/podcast-63-loss-aversion-and-game-design/)).
2. The penalty has to be legible and attributable to player choice, not to the system or to bad luck, or it reads as punishing rather than fair ([Game Wisdom: How Punishment Systems Hurt Game Design](https://game-wisdom.com/critical/punishment-game-design); [arxiv: Struggle as Flow, Soulslikes](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.15318)).
3. Recoverable setbacks (time, currency, buffs) keep tension high without eroding the character. Permanent or semi-permanent setbacks (scars, reputation, relationships) should be rare, meaningful, and often opt-in or narratively earned rather than a universal death tax.
4. Pure permadeath and zero-cost death are both extremes that most successful RPGs avoid for their primary mode; the interesting design space is in the middle, and Aegis's "no permadeath, but death should hurt" brief sits squarely there.

## How Notable Games Solved This

### Dark Souls / Elden Ring: currency-at-risk, not character-at-risk

Mechanic: on death, the player drops all currently-held souls/runes at the death location and respawns at the last rest point (bonfire/Site of Grace) with those souls at zero. The player can walk back to the death spot and reclaim the full amount exactly once; dying again before reclaiming it destroys it permanently ([Gamer Tweak: What Do You Lose When You Die In Elden Ring?](https://gamertweak.com/death-penalty-elden-ring/)). Nothing about the character (stats, gear, quest state) is ever lost. Enemies between the rest point and the corpse typically respawn, so the "corpse run" itself is a real, replayable challenge, not empty backtracking.

Why it works psychologically: the loss is entirely of a fungible, in-run resource (currency), never of the character's identity or invested build. The recovery run gives the player one chance at redemption, which converts a pure loss into a tense but winnable second attempt, this "you can still fix it" window is doing a lot of the emotional work. Research on Soulslike difficulty framing found players display a strong "internal locus of control": they blame their own mistakes (over-greed, impatience) rather than the system, which is what keeps repeated death from feeling unfair ([arxiv: Struggle as Flow: Challenge, Design and Experience in Soulslike Games](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.15318)). Miyazaki has described the explicit design goal as making the trial-and-error loop itself enjoyable rather than treating death as a mark of failure: "If death is to be more than a mark of failure, how do I give it meaning? How do I make death enjoyable?" and he explicitly wants players to "study the game, memorize what's happening, and learn from their mistakes" rather than face arbitrary difficulty ([Frontline Japan, Miyazaki interview](https://www.frontlinejp.net/2022/03/08/discussing-design-philosophy-elden-ring-release-interview-with-director-miyazaki-part-2-2/)).

Key takeaway for a turn-based game: the corpse-run tension relies on real-time positional risk (can you get back there alive?). In a turn-based TUI game this can still work if travel/backtracking has its own encounter risk, but it will feel less visceral without the twitch-skill layer, so the "single reclaim attempt" and "lose it forever on second death" clauses need to carry more of the weight.

### Roguelites: meta-progression converts death into currency for the next run

**Hades**: death is frequent (expected every run) and central to the plot, not a fail state. Each run is short (30 to 60 minutes), and death always converts into narrative progress (new dialogue, relationship beats with House of Hades NPCs) plus meta-currency (Darkness, Keys, Titan Blood, Nectar) that persists and unlocks permanent boons, mirror upgrades, and weapon aspects. In-run resources (money, temporary boons) are lost, and story/meta-currency is banked ([Death Is a Whale: Rogue Legacy essay](https://deathisawhale.com/2021/01/06/rogue-legacy-the-things-we-leave-behind/); [Lords of Gaming: Desiring Death](https://lordsofgaming.net/2021/08/desiring-death-how-hades-changes-the-roguelike-formula/)). The lesson: separate "run-scoped" resources that death should threaten from "meta-scoped" resources that should survive it, and make death itself a content delivery mechanism, not a content lockout.

**Rogue Legacy**: death is permanent for the specific hero you were controlling, but gold collected is banked into the family manor to buy permanent stat upgrades, and the next playable character is a randomly-traited heir who inherits the manor's upgrades. Cellar Door Games explicitly built this "legacy" system to soften the harshness of classic roguelike permadeath while keeping tension inside a single life ([Death Is a Whale essay](https://deathisawhale.com/2021/01/06/rogue-legacy-the-things-we-leave-behind/); [Game Developer: Death in Gaming, Roguelikes and Rogue Legacy](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/death-in-gaming-roguelikes-and-quot-rogue-legacy-quot-)). This is the direct ancestor of "infinite NG+" in the Aegis brief: death (or here, "completing a life") feeds a persistent character-line resource pool rather than zeroing the player out.

Aegis doesn't want to kill the player's actual character on death, so the useful transplant isn't "permadeath + heir," it's the resource-separation logic: run-scoped loot/buffs should be at risk, banked meta-progress (skills learned, key relationships, reputation, unlocked recipes) should not be.

### Injury and scar systems: converting death into a narrative/mechanical scar instead of erasing the character

**Wildermyth**: when a hero would die in combat, the game gives a "mortal choice": die permanently and grant the rest of the party a boon, or survive maimed and withdraw from that battle. Maiming is a genuine, lasting mechanical and visual change, e.g. a lost arm becomes a hook that grants a unique "Hook Gouge" attack but forbids wielding weapons, a lost leg becomes a peg leg that reduces speed, and legacy (returning) heroes who would otherwise lose a limb instead take a permanent (that story) health reduction ([Wildermyth Wiki: Maim](https://wildermyth.com/wiki/Maim); [Steam discussion clarifying legacy maiming rules](https://steamcommunity.com/app/763890/discussions/0/3041607080049224491)). Devs tune the target rate deliberately: about 1 to 4 maimings across a whole campaign, 0 to 2 per chapter, so scars stay rare and story-worthy rather than a grind of accumulating debuffs.

**Kenshi**: limb loss is a live combat outcome (a limb below -100% HP can pop off), and recovery requires making it to a Skeleton Bed to install a robotic prosthetic limb, which comes in varying quality tiers, some of which are strictly better than the organic limb they replace ([Kenshi Wiki: Guide to Health](https://kenshi.fandom.com/wiki/Guide_to_Health); [Kenshi Wiki: Robot Limbs](https://kenshi.fandom.com/wiki/Robot_Limbs)). This is a good "injury without perma-loss" template: a scar is a real mechanical hit, but the game gives an explicit, sometimes upgrade-flavored path back to parity or better, at a resource/time cost.

**Darkest Dungeon**: does not maim on death (dead heroes in Darkest Dungeon are actually gone, closer to Rogue Legacy's model) but its Stress/Affliction system is the best-in-class model for "temporary but very real loss of control" as a consequence of danger. Stress accumulates 0 to 100+; at the threshold a hero rolls an Affliction check, usually failing into a negative Affliction (selfish, paranoid, masochistic, etc.) that causes autonomous, often harmful behavior, or rarely succeeding into a positive Virtue state. Quirks are the slower-building, sometimes-permanent layer: persistent positive/negative traits a hero can accumulate across a campaign. Red Hook's stated design intent: "We wanted to capture the human response to stress. Any person can break under pressure, and people break in different ways," and the designers deliberately wanted players in "uncomfortable situations where they do not have complete control of their party" ([Game Developer: Darkest Dungeon's Affliction System Deep Dive](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/game-design-deep-dive-i-darkest-dungeon-s-i-affliction-system)). The key design lesson: they made the meter visible (they originally wanted it hidden, but playtesting showed players need transparent feedback to feel the punishment is fair) and gave the player town-based recovery actions (drinking, prayer, gambling) with deliberately limited slots, turning stress management into its own strategic puzzle rather than a one-way ratchet.

### XP debt and corpse runs: the MMO cautionary tale

**EverQuest (1999)** set the template that the genre later walked back from: dying dropped your corpse (with your equipped items) at the death location, forced a naked run back to retrieve it, and inflicted an XP loss severe enough that repeated deaths without recovery time could delevel a character ([PC Gamer: Early MMOs came up with the most painful game mechanic of all time](https://www.pcgamer.com/xp-debt-early-mmos-most-painful-game-mechanic/); [Massively OP: Guide to MMORPG death penalties](https://massivelyop.com/2016/03/05/massively-ops-guide-to-death-penalties/)). This was punishing in the bad sense: time-costly, occasionally itemcostly (corpse could be looted or lost in some eras/zones), and could compound into a spiral where a player too undergeared to progress kept dying and losing more ground. EverQuest 2 introduced **XP debt** as a softer replacement: instead of literally losing XP, you go into debt and earn at a reduced rate until it's paid off, so you can never delevel, only slow down ([PC Gamer, same source]). World of Warcraft eventually removed level-loss risk entirely, replacing punishment with a durability/gold hit and a temporary "Resurrection Sickness" debuff, and separately introduced rested XP as a pure carrot instead of a corpse-run stick.

The trajectory across the genre (naked corpse run + XP loss to XP debt to trivial durability tax) is itself the strongest evidence in this research: the industry converged, over ~15 years, on softer death penalties as the ceiling for what feels acceptable in a persistent, primarily PvE single-character game. Aegis, as a single-player game without other players to race or grief, has more room to reintroduce some of that harsher tension without the systemic unfairness that made XP loss hated in a multiplayer economy (there's no guild raid waiting on you, no other player looting your corpse).

### Durability loss on death: a small, bounded tax

Diablo III and Diablo IV both use a flat, small equipped-item durability hit per death (roughly 10 percent, or -10 durability, with unequipped inventory items untouched), which is a bounded, self-limiting penalty since a full break just means a repair-gold cost, not item destruction, unless the player lets it go all the way to 0 ([Diablo Wiki: Durability](https://www.diablowiki.net/Durability); [Game8: Diablo 4 Death Penalty Explained](https://game8.co/games/Diablo-4/archives/414517)). Albion Online deliberately avoided durability loss for its open-world PvP death penalty and used item destruction chance instead, specifically because durability loss encouraged players to intentionally gear into low-durability junk to game the system ([Albion Online forums, item loss mechanics](https://forum.albiononline.com/index.php/Thread/1711-Death-Item-Loss-Mechanics/?pageNo=2)), a good example of a penalty that looks reasonable on paper but creates a degenerate optimal play pattern in practice.

**Diablo II Hardcore vs Softcore** is a useful bookend: Hardcore is true permadeath (character and all its gear gone forever, party members who are set to "loot" can salvage the equipped gear off the corpse), while Softcore just costs a level/gold-scaled percentage of gold plus (at higher difficulties) some XP ([PureDiablo D2 Wiki: Hardcore](https://www.purediablo.com/d2wiki/Hardcore); [Diablo Fandom: Hardcore](https://diablo.fandom.com/wiki/Hardcore)). The fact that Blizzard ships both modes side by side, identical simulation, only mortality changed, is itself a validated pattern: let the same content support radically different stakes without redesigning the game.

## Pitfalls and Failure Modes

- **Spiral/snowball penalties**: EverQuest's XP loss could delevel an already-struggling player, making them weaker and more likely to die again. Any Aegis penalty that reduces core combat capability (not just convenience/currency) risks a death spiral for exactly the players who can least afford it. Prefer penalties that cost time or resources over penalties that reduce power.
- **Invisible or illegible penalties feel unfair**: Darkest Dungeon's own postmortem notes they had to make the stress meter visible after playtesting showed hidden mechanics read as arbitrary punishment. Any Aegis death penalty must be shown to the player in advance (a status effect, a countdown, a clearly named debuff), not discovered after the fact.
- **Degenerate optimization around the penalty**: Albion Online's durability-loss version taught players to gear cheaply to minimize loss, defeating the system's intent. Watch for any Aegis penalty that a rational player can trivially route around by hoarding worthless gear or avoiding progression.
- **Corpse runs without real risk become busywork**: if the return trip to reclaim lost resources is trivial (no enemies, no time pressure), it's padding, not tension. If it's too risky, it becomes an unfair second death that compounds the first.
- **Permanent debuffs stacking without a release valve**: Darkest Dungeon deliberately bounds Affliction/Quirk accumulation with town recovery actions; a system that only adds scars and never offers a (costly, slow) path to remove or offset them will make veteran characters unplayable rather than characterful, and will punish long play sessions disproportionately.
- **Reputation/relationship penalties are the easiest to make feel unfair**, because the player often can't fully evaluate the social cost before choosing an action. If Aegis attaches reputation loss to death (e.g., dying in front of witnesses, failing to protect an NPC), the consequence needs to be legible before or during the moment of risk, not sprung on the player afterward.
- **Save-scumming undermines any death penalty that isn't enforced by the save architecture itself.** A penalty that a player can trivially undo by reloading isn't a penalty, it's a false threat that erodes trust in the whole system once discovered. See the "save integration" section below.

## Death and the Save System (Anti-Save-Scumming)

This is the piece that has no direct precedent in most of the above games, because Dark Souls/Elden Ring don't have manual saves at all (the game autosaves continuously and there's exactly one save slot per character, so the death penalty cannot be undone by reloading), and classic roguelikes enforce permadeath by deleting the save file on death. Aegis, as a non-permadeath single-player RPG with presumably a normal save/load flow, needs a deliberate answer or the whole death-setback system is optional.

Patterns from the field ([RimWorld/XCOM2/HOI4 Ironman discussions](https://steamcommunity.com/app/294100/discussions/0/1798529872659277177/); [OpenMW Ironman/Permadeath forum thread](https://forum.openmw.org/viewtopic.php?t=8122)):

- **Single-slot autosave-only ("Ironman") mode**: the game autosaves on a cadence and/or at key events, and manual save/load is disabled or heavily restricted, so a death's consequences are written to disk before the player can react. This is the strongest anti-scum measure but is usually offered as an optional mode (XCOM 2, Hearts of Iron IV, Crusader Kings/Paradox titles) rather than forced, because some players legitimately want save-scum as their preferred difficulty.
- **The autosave-before-consequence trick**: autosave immediately upon death (before showing the penalty screen) rather than only at level start/checkpoints, so reloading "the last save" reloads into the post-death state, not a pristine pre-death state. This is close to how Souls games behave by construction.
- **Accept that scumming exists and design the penalty to not be worth reversing.** If death costs are mild, time-based, and diegetic (a scar, an in-world debuff, a bit of lost loot) rather than catastrophic, most players won't bother save-scumming for the same reason people don't reload after a bad-but-recoverable trade in a game like Slay the Spire: the friction of reloading (losing later, unrelated progress) exceeds the annoyance of the penalty.
- **Practical middle ground for Aegis**: offer normal manual saves for casual players, but (a) make the autosave-on-death write immediately and be the default "continue" target, (b) put the truly consequence-bearing state (reputation, world-state flags, relationship damage) in the autosave-on-death write so a naive reload doesn't erase it, and (c) offer an explicit optional "Aegis Ironman/hardcore" toggle for players who want the tension enforced, mirroring the Diablo II Hardcore/Softcore split and the Paradox Ironman pattern, rather than trying to force one universal answer.

## NG+ Scaling of Death Setbacks

Traditional NG+ implementations (Dark Souls, Elden Ring, Lies of P) primarily scale enemy stats (health/damage, commonly cited around a 30 percent to 40 percent multiplier increase per cycle, tapering off after several cycles, e.g. Dark Souls stops adding difficulty past NG+7) while keeping the player's build and item drops mostly intact ([Fextralife: Elden Ring NG+ Guide](https://eldenring.wiki.fextralife.com/New+Game+Plus); [Dark Souls Fandom: New Game Plus](https://darksouls.fandom.com/wiki/New_Game_Plus)). None of the researched precedents scale the *death penalty itself* across NG+ tiers in a well-documented way, this appears to be an open design space rather than a solved one, which is worth flagging directly to the Aegis design team as a place to innovate rather than copy.

Given the rest of this research, a coherent approach for infinite NG+ scaling would be:

- Keep the *shape* of the death penalty identical across NG+ tiers (players should never have to relearn what death does), but scale its *magnitude and reach*: longer recovery corpse-runs, larger currency/time losses, a higher chance that a death produces a scar/quirk instead of a purely recoverable penalty, and reputation consequences that spread further (more NPCs hear about it, harsher faction reactions).
- Consider making the permanent-ish consequences (scars, quirks) the primary NG+ difficulty knob rather than raw numeric scaling, since procedurally generated worlds already give infinite content variety, the marginal challenge NG+ needs to add is consequence weight, not just bigger numbers. This also reinforces the "no two playthroughs the same" pillar: your NG+3 character's accumulated scars and reputation become part of what makes that run distinct.

## Recommendations for Aegis

1. **Split every loss into "run-scoped" (recoverable) vs "banked" (never lost to death) resources, and be explicit about which bucket everything sits in.** Core stats, learned skills, owned equipment, and quest/relationship progress should be in the "banked, never lost" bucket (this is your no-permadeath promise). Unspent currency, unbanked loot from the current expedition, and short-duration buffs should be "at risk," recoverable via a Souls-style single-chance corpse run (with real, replayable danger along the return path, not a freebie), forfeited permanently on a second death before recovery.

2. **Make the primary death penalty a bounded, diegetic combination of time cost, small currency/loot risk, and a temporary debuff, not a stat-permanent or level-permanent loss.** EverQuest's XP-loss/delevel spiral and the industry's subsequent retreat from it (XP debt, then near-zero penalty in WoW) is the strongest single lesson from this research: penalties that reduce a struggling player's power make them more likely to die again, which reads as unfair rather than tense. Favor an EQ2-style "debt" framing over an EQ1-style "loss" framing wherever the two are options: for Aegis this might mean a temporary "Shaken" or "Wounded" condition that reduces effectiveness for a bounded time/number of encounters and can be worked off, rather than an outright stat or XP subtraction.

3. **Reserve genuinely permanent-ish consequences (scars, injuries, reputation stains) for a small, tunable minority of deaths, gated behind a specific trigger, not every death.** Take Wildermyth's target rate (roughly one to a handful of maimings across an entire campaign) as your calibration anchor rather than "a scar every death." A workable trigger: only deaths against a named/boss-tier threat, or deaths where the player was already critically low and "should have retreated," escalate to a scar/reputation hit; routine deaths to trash mobs stay in the purely recoverable bucket. Give every scar a Kenshi-style *path back to parity* (a costly, time-gated repair, a quest, a shrine, an expensive prosthetic-equivalent) so scars add flavor and difficulty without becoming an unbounded downward ratchet, and pair them with Darkest Dungeon-style visible meters and recovery actions so the player always sees the mechanic coming and has agency to manage it.

4. **Treat the save system as part of the death design, not an afterthought: autosave immediately on death, before showing the penalty, and write all consequence state (reputation flags, scars, world-state changes) into that autosave.** Offer manual saves for players who want them, since forcing Ironman on everyone will alienate a chunk of your audience, but ship an explicit optional "Ironman"-style toggle (mirroring Diablo II Hardcore/Softcore and Paradox's Ironman mode) for players who want the tension enforced by the save architecture itself rather than by self-discipline. Keep ordinary penalties mild enough (per recommendation 2) that most players won't feel compelled to reload past them anyway; the goal is a system that stays honest even when it's cheap.

5. **Scale NG+ death consequences by widening reach and raising the odds of a "scarring" outcome, not by bolting on new penalty types.** Keep the shape of death constant across every NG+ tier (players who've internalized "what happens when I die" in NG+0 shouldn't have to relearn it), but in higher tiers: shrink the corpse-run recovery window, extend debuff durations, increase the probability that a qualifying death produces a scar/quirk instead of a purely recoverable penalty, and let reputation consequences propagate to more NPCs/factions. This makes NG+ difficulty partly about consequence-weight rather than pure enemy-stat inflation, which better fits a procedurally generated, narrative-forward game than the Souls approach of just multiplying enemy health.

6. **Use death as a story generator, not just a punishment, borrowing Hades's trick of banking narrative alongside currency.** Every death is an opportunity for Aegis's authored-feeling narrative layer to react: an NPC who saw you fall remembers it, a scar becomes a hook for later dialogue, a reputation hit opens a new quest thread (someone offers to help you recover what you lost, for a price). This converts the setback from pure loss into content, directly supporting the "authored narrative inside a procedural world" pillar, and gives death emotional texture beyond a numeric tax.
