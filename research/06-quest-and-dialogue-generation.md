# Procedural Quest and Dialogue Generation: Avoiding the "Radiant Quest" Problem

## Overview

The "radiant quest" problem, named after Skyrim's Radiant Story system, is what happens when a quest generator produces content that is structurally valid but narratively empty: fetch this, kill that, deliver the other thing, with the nouns swapped out of a template each time. Players notice within a few repetitions because the generator is only filling slots (location, enemy type, reward) without any underlying causal story: no reason the quest exists, no memory that it happened, no consequence that persists. The quest is generated fresh each time it triggers rather than referencing anything true about the world (see UESP's own writeup on Skyrim's Radiant system and the academic critique in the IST Lisbon thesis on improving it: https://en.uesp.net/wiki/Skyrim:Radiant, https://labjogos.ist.utl.pt/en/thesis/procedural-quest-generation-improving-skyrim-s-radiant-story).

The games and research that actually solve this do not abandon procedural generation, they change what gets procedurally generated. Instead of generating the *plot*, they generate the *cast, history, and world state*, then run authored (or at least authored-feeling) templates over that state. The throughline across every successful example below is: **write the structure by hand, fill the particulars from a simulation with memory.**

This document surveys the concrete mechanisms behind that idea, in roughly increasing order of "how much of the story is handwritten" to "how much is simulated."

## Academic foundations: quest patterns and dependency graphs

The oldest rigorous attempt to formalize quest structure is a line of academic work analyzing hundreds of quests from live MMORPGs to extract a taxonomy. One frequently cited structural analysis reviewed over 750 quests across four MMORPGs and derived a common grammar of quest components (givers, objectives, rewards, locations) that could recombine (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/254003504_A_prototype_quest_generator_based_on_a_structural_analysis_of_quests_from_four_MMORPGs). A related design-patterns paper, "Situating Quests," catalogued around 33 quest design patterns and 57 level design patterns drawn from over 20 RPGs, arguing that quests are combinations of a small vocabulary of moves (fetch, escort, kill, discover, deliver) layered with framing devices (https://www.academia.edu/2806300/Situating_quests_design_patterns_for_quest_and_level_design_in_role_playing_games). Ian Parberry's research group at UNT built quest generators directly on this taxonomy (https://ianparberry.com/research/quests/), and a widely cited AAAI paper, "Quest Patterns for Story-Based Computer Games," formalizes 17 meta quest points as a pattern library others can combine into longer arcs (https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/12408/12408-52-15936-1-2-20201228.pdf).

The key lesson from this body of work is not the taxonomy itself (fetch/kill/escort patterns are old news to any designer), it is the *dependency graph* idea: individual quests are nodes, and edges encode prerequisite/unlock/consequence relationships, so a generator is choosing a path or subgraph through a pre-authored plot-point graph rather than inventing content from nothing. A 2026 paper, "From World-Gen to Quest-Line: A Dependency-Driven Prompt Pipeline for Coherent RPG Generation" (https://arxiv.org/html/2604.25482v1), makes this explicit for LLM-driven generation: each generation stage is conditioned on the complete set of structured JSON outputs from all preceding stages, quests reference NPCs/locations/factions by ID rather than inventing new ones, and inter-quest dependencies are encoded as structured references between quest objects. The paper is explicit about the failure mode this avoids: independent, single-prompt-per-quest generation produces "internal inconsistencies, shallow narrative logic, and limited integration with structured game systems," while campaign-level batch generation with explicit ID references keeps quests from contradicting each other or hallucinating entities that don't exist in world state.

This is directly applicable to Aegis: whatever plot-point graph or quest-pattern library you author, the generator's job should be graph traversal and slot-filling against a world-state database with stable IDs, not free generation.

## Storylets and quality-based narrative (Failbetter, StoryNexus, Emily Short)

Failbetter Games (Fallen London, Sunless Sea) invented the term "quality-based narrative" (QBN) to describe a structure built entirely from storylets: small chunks of authored content, each gated by prerequisite conditions on numeric "qualities" (skills, reputation, inventory, story-progress flags), each with effects that change those qualities afterward (Emily Short's canonical definition and StoryNexus writing: https://emshort.blog/2019/11/29/storylets-you-want-them/, https://videlais.github.io/simple-qbn/qbn.html). Unlike a branching tree, storylets are not connected to each other by authored links; they are indexed by their prerequisites and surfaced whenever the world state satisfies them. This means the *set* of available storylets at any moment is emergent from world state even though every individual storylet is fully handwritten prose.

Short's key craft points, distilled from her blog (see also "Beyond Branching," https://emshort.blog/2016/04/12/beyond-branching-quality-based-and-salience-based-narrative-structures/, and Max Kreminski's academic survey of the design space, "Sketching a Map of the Storylets Design Space," https://www.researchgate.net/publication/329067385_Sketching_a_Map_of_the_Storylets_Design_Space, discussed at https://emshort.blog/2019/01/06/kreminski-on-storylets/):

- Storylets are "simple, atomic, robust, and recombinable." Write them as small, self-contained units, not chapters.
- The failure mode to avoid is the "time cave": fully branching content that never recombines, which causes combinatorial explosion in authoring cost. Storylets solve this because many different qualities/paths converge into the *same* storylet, and the story state fans back in rather than only fanning out.
- Content quality problems appear early ("uninteresting until a fair number of storylets exist"), meaning a v1 storylet database will feel thin no matter how good the individual writing is; density is what sells the illusion of a living world, not any one clever storylet.
- Generalize the *rule* that unlocks a storylet rather than hand-tuning one-off conditions per storylet, so that new world content (new NPCs, new factions) automatically becomes eligible to trigger existing storylets without new authoring.

Kreminski's more recent work, "Drama Llama" (https://arxiv.org/html/2501.09099), combines LLM generation with the storylet structure specifically to solve the authoring-scale problem: the storylet skeleton (preconditions, effects, slots) stays human authored and machine-checked, while an LLM fills in the prose variation within each storylet, keeping quality-control the structural part while getting infinite textual variety on the surface.

## Wildermyth: authored scenes, procedurally cast characters

Wildermyth is probably the cleanest existing example of "authored template, procedurally cast particulars" in a full narrative RPG. Campaigns are structured as a "string of pearls": a sequence of authored story beats (the "pearls") interleaved with procedurally generated tactical battles (the "string") (see https://cjleo.com/blog/the-power-of-wildermyths-modular-storytelling-in-game-design/ and coverage in Vice: https://www.vice.com/en/article/pkbz78/wildermyth-review). Designers lock in required story beats for the beginning, middle, and end of a campaign, and fill the space between them with a chosen number of optional modular scenes, so every campaign has a guaranteed narrative spine but a variable amount of connective tissue.

Crucially, the scenes themselves are written by human authors *without knowing which specific characters will appear in them*. A scene calls for roles ("a character with a rivalry," "the party's most cowardly member," "two characters with an unresolved romance flag") and the game slots in whichever current party members satisfy those role constraints, using each character's accumulated traits, relationship qualities, and personality choices to color how the scene's dialogue and outcome plays out (https://www.superjumpmagazine.com/wordplayer-wildermyth-lays-bare-the-false-promises-of-ai-storytelling/). This is functionally identical to the storylet model above (preconditions = role requirements, effects = trait/relationship changes) but delivered as illustrated "comic panel" scenes rather than prose, which disguises the templating even further.

Two other mechanics worth stealing directly:
- **Legacy across playthroughs**: characters who survive a campaign graduate into a persistent "legacy" pool and can be recruited as elder mentors, ancestors, or cameo NPCs in future campaigns, so procedurally generated player-created characters become the raw material for *future* authored-feeling content, not just this run's story.
- **Author credit as flavor**: each scene displays which human writer authored it. This is a cheap trick but it reinforces the feeling that the world is stitched from real short stories, not spat out of a black box.

## Caves of Qud: history generation feeding faction and dialogue behavior

Caves of Qud generates roughly the reverse of Wildermyth's approach: instead of authoring scenes and casting characters into them, it generates a deep, causally connected *history* first, and lets NPC behavior and dialogue read off that history as ground truth. At world generation, the game creates a set of historical events and relationships centered on five randomly generated ancient rulers ("Sultans"), consciously modeled on Dwarf Fortress's and Epitaph's history generators (https://articles.retroware.com/2020/11/18/procedurally-generated-history/, https://wiki.cavesofqud.com/wiki/World_generation). Every village generated later in the game is a "culmination" of that history: it derives its name, government type, religion, architecture, cuisine, local dialect/lexicon, and relationships with neighboring villages from the branching history tree, and it is populated with named NPCs and locally-flavored quests consistent with that derivation (https://aidanpage.medium.com/generating-anything-and-everything-in-caves-of-qud-d6336e9afda0, https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/tapping-into-the-potential-of-procedural-generation-in-caves-of-qud).

The concrete payoff example the developers cite: a village whose historical founder championed crab rights centuries earlier may refuse to trade with a player who has been cruel to crustaceans, or send the player on a quest to retrieve books about crabs. The dialogue and quest content is not written for "a village," it is written as a *function of that village's specific derived history*, so the same quest pattern ("retrieve a lost text") reads as bespoke because the topic, NPC motivations, and faction attitudes are all traceable to a specific, game-unique historical event.

For pure text flavor (not structural content), Qud layers a Markov-chain generator trained on a small, deliberately narrow corpus (its own help text plus two out-of-copyright 19th-century books) to produce "lingual drift," text that is thematically on-key but syntactically fractured, standing in for millennia of oral/textual decay. This is a good example of using a cheap generative technique *only* where authenticity of meaning doesn't matter (ancient scrap-text flavor), while keeping the load-bearing content (quest logic, faction attitudes) fully structured and deterministic.

## Shadow of Mordor / Shadow of War: the Nemesis system as NPC memory

The Nemesis system is the strongest existing example of procedural NPCs with persistent memory driving dialogue. Every orc captain tracks a history of encounters with the player (defeats, being humiliated, fleeing, killing an ally), and future dialogue and behavior is generated by referencing that specific history rather than a generic template (GDC talk "Helping Players Hate (or Love) Their Nemesis," https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1025150/Helping-Players-Hate-(or-Love); overview at https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-i-shadow-of-mordor-i-s-nemesis-system). Mechanically: traits and rank are randomly assigned and can change based on outcomes (an orc who defeats the player is promoted and scarred, gaining a bragging line referencing that exact fight); the "vengeance" mechanic guarantees narratively-motivated recurrence, an orc who beat you *will* show up again, seeking you out, rather than the world simply forgetting the encounter.

The postmortem-style retrospective in Game Developer ("The Nemesis of Narrative," https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/the-nemesis-of-narrative) is worth noting for its honesty about limits: the underlying patent literature explicitly frames prior systems (branching dialogue trees) as unable to "provide a realistic degree of complexity that fits in appropriately with the flow of game play," and the fix was to consult a database of past interactions to select the closest-matching line, not to generate genuinely new text. In other words, even the best-regarded procedural-memory system in the industry still relies on a large bank of handwritten voice lines (hundreds per orc) selected by matching against memory state; the "AI" is a smart selector over authored content, not a language generator. That is a feature, not a limitation, to copy: memory-driven line *selection* against a big authored bank is more reliable and controllable than open generation.

## Simulation-driven emergence: Dwarf Fortress and RimWorld

Dwarf Fortress generates up to 500 years of world history before play even starts (Legends mode), simulating individual historical figures, civilizations, wars, and artifacts in enough mechanical detail that genuinely novel causal chains occur (the frequently cited example: a jilted historical figure obsessed with mortality who spends seventy years building an army of undead, one of whom eventually kills her former lover in battle decades later) (https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/DF2014:Legends, https://if50.substack.com/p/2006-dwarf-fortress). Nothing about that story was authored; it fell out of modeling relationships, aging, magic, and war as interacting systems over a long simulated timescale. The lesson for Aegis is that narrative "specificity" does not require an author at all if the simulation is rich and long-running enough, but the cost is that Dwarf Fortress's history is illegible without the player digging through Legends mode; the game does very little work to *surface* these stories as dialogue or quests. It's a cautionary tale about generating rich history that never gets narratively delivered to the player.

RimWorld inverts the emphasis: rather than simulating history for its own sake, its "AI Storyteller" is explicitly a pacing director, an algorithm that selects events from a categorized incident database based on current colony state (population, wealth, recent event history, mood) to keep tension in a target band, deliberately injuring rather than killing, easing off after low population, escalating after quiet stretches (GDC talk "RimWorld: Contrarian, Ridiculous, and Impossible Game Design Methods," https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1024232/-RimWorld-Contrarian-Ridiculous-and, slides at https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2017/Presentations/Sylvester_Tynan_RimWorld_Contrarian_Ridiculous.pdf). Sylvester's framing, explicit in the talk, is that RimWorld was designed "not as a game but as a story generator": every system (mood breaks, raids, relationships, permadeath of colonists) exists specifically to produce anecdotes worth retelling, and the storyteller's only job is pacing which systemic events fire and when, not authoring their content. This is a useful complementary layer to storylets/quests: a pacing director that decides *when* to surface which category of pre-authored content, tuned against player/world state, rather than a system that invents content.

## Dialogue templating with personality, faction, and history variables

Below the level of whole quests, the same principle applies to individual lines of dialogue: template the *sentence structure*, fill slots from state, and vary tone/word-choice by persistent variables rather than regenerating meaning from scratch. GearHead RPG's "Caramel" dialogue system is a good small-scale reference implementation: it uses a token-expansion grammar where the dialogue processor recursively replaces tokens in a template against a grammar dictionary until no tokens remain, and the token choices are conditioned on personality traits, so the same underlying "reason to say this" (asking for a favor, threatening, greeting) reads differently depending on who's speaking (https://www.gearheadrpg.com/2019/06/12/caramel-dialogue-real-procedural-personalities/). More broadly, industry writing on this pattern (e.g. https://www.leerebelwriters.com/how-dynamic-npc-dialogue-systems-create-meaningful-player-relationships-in-games/) converges on the same variable set worth tracking per NPC: mood/personality trait vector, faction/allegiance, career or role, and a rolling memory of specific player interactions, with dialogue selection/generation keyed off all four simultaneously rather than any single one. Academic work on LLM-driven approaches (e.g. PSYDIAL, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.00930, and "Ontologically Faithful Generation of Non-Player Character Dialogue," https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.10618) is converging on the same idea from the generative side: constrain the model with a structured ontology/knowledge graph of world facts so lines stay grounded in true state rather than the model inventing plausible-sounding but false claims (the generative-AI version of the radiant quest problem: fluent but hollow, or worse, fluent but *wrong*).

## Pitfalls and failure modes (cross-cutting)

- **Slot-filling without causality** (classic radiant quest failure): quests reference randomized nouns but nothing about *why* this NPC wants this item from this location. Fix: every generated quest instance must be traceable to a specific fact in world state (a grudge, a shortage, a historical event), not just a randomly rolled location/enemy/reward triple.
- **Combinatorial time-caves**: fully bespoke branching per NPC/quest is unsustainable at scale; content quality is bad until the storylet/pattern database is large, and large databases are expensive to hand-author. Budget for a long "unimpressive middle" period during development and design around convergence (many paths reconverging into shared storylets) rather than pure branching.
- **Memory without delivery**: Dwarf Fortress shows that a rich simulated history is worthless narratively if the game doesn't surface it back to the player through legible dialogue, quest text, or UI. Simulation depth is necessary but not sufficient; you also need an explicit "narration layer" that reads simulation state and turns it into sentences a player will actually see.
- **Selection dressed as generation, done badly**: Nemesis-style memory-driven line selection needs a large enough bank of authored variants per situation, or repetition becomes obvious fast (hundreds of voice lines per orc in Shadow of Mordor was itself a large art/budget cost). Underinvesting in bank size while overinvesting in the selection logic reproduces the "mad-libs" feel from the opposite direction: correct topic, robotic delivery.
- **Faction/personality variables that don't compound**: dialogue systems that key off only one variable (just mood, or just faction) read as shallow quickly; the token-expansion / ontology-grounded approaches above only feel specific because multiple variables combine (personality x faction x memory x current world fact) in the same line.
- **Losing world coherence at scale in LLM pipelines**: independent per-quest or per-NPC generation calls (no shared state) reliably produce contradictions (arxiv 2604.25482 above). Any LLM-assisted content generation for Aegis needs a shared, ID-referenced world-state document passed into every generation call, and validation that referenced entities actually exist.

## Recommendations for Aegis

1. **Model the world as a fact/relationship graph first, quests second.** Build the same kind of history/faction/relationship generator Caves of Qud uses (settlements, factions, notable NPCs, grudges, shortages, past events) as the actual source of truth, and treat quest generation as a *query* over that graph ("find an NPC with an unresolved grudge against a faction the player has standing with") rather than a generator that invents its own justification. This is the single highest-leverage fix for hollowness: if a quest cannot be traced to a specific fact already in world state, don't generate it.

2. **Use storylets as the quest/dialogue content unit, not scripts or dialogue trees.** Author quest and dialogue content as small, atomic, precondition-gated chunks (Failbetter/StoryNexus model) indexed by generalized rules against world-state variables (faction standing, region history, NPC memory flags, player stats/skills), so new procedurally generated world content automatically becomes eligible to trigger existing authored content without new authoring work. Budget for the "uninteresting until dense" scaling curve Emily Short describes; plan a large initial storylet library before expecting emergent variety to read as rich.

3. **Adopt Wildermyth's role-casting pattern for major quest scenes.** Write pivotal quest beats (not radiant filler) as scenes with role slots ("an NPC who owes the player a debt," "the local faction leader," "an NPC with high suspicion of the player") rather than named characters, and cast current world-state NPCs into those roles at generation time. This gets handwritten-quality prose and pacing for the moments that matter, while still varying who fills them run to run.

4. **Give every notable NPC a persistent memory log, and make dialogue selection read it directly (Nemesis model).** Track a structured, queryable history per NPC (defeats, favors, betrayals, promises made and kept/broken) and select/compose dialogue lines by matching against that log rather than only checking current stats. Invest in a moderately large bank of line variants per situation type, personality-flavored, so memory-driven selection doesn't read as robotic once the "right topic" is found.

5. **Separate the pacing director from the content generator.** Build a RimWorld-style storyteller layer that decides *when* to surface which category of quest/event based on recent pacing (time since last major beat, current danger level, region tension), distinct from the systems that decide *what* the content is. This keeps quest density and dramatic rhythm tunable independently of the world-state/storylet machinery, and gives NG+ a clean lever (tighten pacing, raise event severity thresholds) without having to regenerate quest content logic.

6. **If using LLMs for prose generation, always pass a shared, ID-referenced world-state snapshot into every call and validate references afterward.** Never let an LLM invent quest content in a vacuum per NPC or per quest; condition it on the same structured JSON world-state object recent research uses (arxiv 2604.25482), and reject/regenerate any output that references an NPC, location, or faction not present in that snapshot. This keeps the "authored template, procedural cast" property even when generation is offloaded to a model rather than handwritten storylets.
