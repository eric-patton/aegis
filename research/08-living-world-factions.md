# Living-World Faction Simulation in Single-Player RPGs

## Overview

"Living world" is sold to players as a promise: factions have goals, they act on those goals whether or not you are watching, and your actions ripple through a web of relationships you don't fully control. In practice almost no shipped game runs a full agent-based simulation that the player experiences directly. What ships is one of three architectures, often blended:

1. **Full background simulation**: agents (armies, traders, settlements) tick continuously with real state, whether or not the player is nearby. Kenshi's world, X4's economy, and Dwarf Fortress's world-gen history are the closest real examples.
2. **Event-driven / storylet simulation**: a lighter model where faction state is a handful of variables and scripted "world states" fire on triggers (an NPC dies, a threshold crosses, a timer elapses). Kenshi's actual shipped faction takeovers, CK3's event chains, and STALKER's shipped (non-A-Life-2.0) faction wars fall here.
3. **Scripted escalation dressed as simulation**: a curated sequence of set-piece beats that *look* reactive but are authored, gated by simple counters. Shadow of War's Nemesis system is this at its best; most "reactive world" AAA RPGs (Fallout, Skyrim, Witcher) use variants of it for faction reputation.

The core finding across postmortems, GDC talks, and dev blogs is that architecture 1 is expensive to build, expensive to debug, and mostly invisible to players unless a huge amount of separate UI/narration work surfaces it. Architecture 2 gets 80% of the perceived aliveness for a fraction of the cost, because what players actually perceive is not the simulation, it's the *narration of state changes*. Architecture 3, done well (Nemesis, RimWorld's storyteller), is frequently rated as *more* alive than true simulations, because every beat is dramatically shaped rather than being simulation noise.

For a solo/small-team project like Aegis, with a hard requirement for full determinism and no runtime LLM, and a world fact/relationship graph as the narrative backbone, the right target is architecture 2 with occasional architecture-3 set pieces layered on top for memorable NPCs, throttled by a RimWorld-style pacing director. Full agent simulation (architecture 1) is explicitly not recommended; see Pitfalls and Recommendations below.

## How Notable Games Solved This

### Kenshi: world states and town overrides (mostly scripted, not simulated)

Kenshi has a reputation as *the* living-sandbox game, but its actual base-game faction dynamism is narrower than its reputation suggests. Town ownership changes are largely pre-authored "world states": killing a specific named leader NPC (sometimes in a different city than the town itself) flips a flag that swaps the town's owning faction and repopulates its garrison and shop rosters. There is no in-game UI pointing you at these triggers, you learn them from the wiki or by experimentation, which is itself a design choice: discovery through play rather than a quest log. The Steam Workshop mod "Living World" (https://steamcommunity.com/workshop/filedetails/?id=1772722749) exists specifically because the base game's world-state coverage is sparse, and it adds many more trigger/override pairs so that more of the world reacts to player and NPC-driven change. Source: https://kenshi.fandom.com/wiki/World_States and https://kenshi.fandom.com/wiki/Changing_Town_Ownership.

The lesson: Kenshi's perceived "living-ness" comes from (a) a genuinely simulated economy and squad-level AI at the moment-to-moment level (patrols, hunger, trade caravans), stacked on top of (b) a comparatively small, hand-authored table of discrete world-state transitions for the macro (who owns what). Players conflate the two and credit the macro layer with a simulation depth it doesn't actually have.

### Mount & Blade (Warband/Bannerlord): dynamic kingdom warfare and its stalemate failure mode

Bannerlord runs a genuine multi-faction war/diplomacy simulation: kingdoms accrue a "war score" from battles and settlement captures, decide war/peace based on treasury, army strength, and relations, and AI lords independently raise armies and besiege settlements. The publicized failure mode is the **endless stalemate war**: once two kingdoms are geographically distant or a faction has no more fiefs to lose, the war-score inputs stop moving, so the AI's declare-peace evaluation never crosses its threshold, and wars persist for in-game decades doing nothing but occasionally raiding villages. Community diagnosis (Steam discussions, mod changelogs) settles on the same root cause repeatedly: the model conditions peace on cost/benefit thresholds that assume active conflict, and has no fallback timer or forced-resolution mechanism for a war that has gone inert. The practical result players report: a defeated, fiefless kingdom that mechanically cannot be fully eliminated and cannot make peace, so it just perpetually harasses. Multiple mods ("War and Peace," "No War For The Defeated," "AI Influence") exist purely to patch this by adding tribute logic, forced peace-on-elimination, or better AI diplomacy weighting. Sources: https://www.nexusmods.com/mountandblade2bannerlord/mods/4759, https://www.nexusmods.com/mountandblade2bannerlord/mods/2820, https://steamcommunity.com/app/261550/discussions/0/691997369114369876/.

The lesson: any simulated war/diplomacy system needs an explicit, designed *exit condition* (forced peace, faction dissolution, tribute/vassalization) independent of the organic score, or it will find a degenerate steady state and sit there, which is far worse for perceived aliveness than a simpler system, because players notice a broken stalemate much more than they'd notice a lack of grand war simulation at all.

### Shadow of Mordor / Shadow of War: the Nemesis system and fortress sieges (scripted escalation, not simulation)

The Nemesis system is architecture 3 done at a AAA level: a roster of procedurally generated orc captains with randomized body parts, voice, strengths/weaknesses, and a persistent memory of every encounter with the player (killed you, was killed and came back scarred, was humiliated, etc.), promoted up a fixed rank hierarchy (captain to warchief to overlord) by scripted rules, not an economic or territorial simulation. GDC 2018 talk by Chris Hoge (Monolith) frames the design goal explicitly as maximizing *player emotion* (hate, respect, fear) rather than simulation fidelity: https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1025150/Helping-Players-Hate-(or-Love).

Shadow of War layers a fortress/siege meta-game on top: each fortress has an Overlord and 2-6 Warchiefs each guarding a capture point with bodyguards; sieges are attacker-vs-defender objective captures (take all points, then challenge the Overlord), and losing a capture point on defense triggers a scripted reinforcement (another warchief breaks in with troops). This is a clean example of a *discrete, bounded, replayable state machine* (N capture points, ranked defenders) producing the feeling of large-scale war without simulating armies, logistics, or terrain. Source: https://shadowofwar.fandom.com/wiki/Fortress, https://www.neoseeker.com/middle-earth-shadow-of-war/Siege_Defense.

The lesson: named-individual memory (who beat me, who I beat, who came back changed) is what makes players *feel* a faction is alive, far more than aggregate territory numbers. It's cheap to fake at the level of a dozen tracked NPCs and expensive to fake at the level of thousands.

### X4 Foundations and Kenshi-style background economy sims

X4 genuinely simulates its galactic economy: miners feed factories, factories build ships and stations, and factions wage territorial war using resources produced by that same economy; if the player destroys civilian shipping the faction's own supply chain visibly degrades. Factions can gain or lose territory entirely offscreen, before the player ever visits that sector. Source: Steam discussions on X4 faction AI and economy, e.g. https://steamcommunity.com/app/392160/discussions/0/1743353164088150092/. This is the purest architecture-1 example in the set: a real economic graph with emergent winners and losers. The tradeoff, per player reports, is that the simulation needs a very long runway (hours of real economy ramp-up) before it produces interesting outcomes, and exploitable gaps in the simulated supply chain are common because balancing thousands of interacting agents is an ongoing tuning problem, not a one-time design task.

### Dwarf Fortress: world-gen history feeding adventure mode

Dwarf Fortress generates centuries of history before the player ever starts a game: civilizations rise, fight wars, found and lose sites, produce named historical figures, and this entire history is queryable in "Legends mode." Adventure mode then drops the player into the *result* of that history (sacked fortresses, standing grudges, famous heroes) rather than simulating it live while you play. Source: https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/World_generation, https://www.dwarffortresswiki.org/Adventure_mode_quick_start.

This is structurally close to what Aegis already committed to (a world fact/relationship graph generated at worldgen via causal grammars): DF proves the pattern works and that a pre-baked causal history read by the player during play, rather than simulated live in front of them, is enough to produce "this world has a past" perception. The expensive, failure-prone part of DF's approach (thousands of live-simulated agents during actual fortress/adventure play, notoriously the source of DF's legendary performance problems) is exactly the part Aegis should *not* copy; the worldgen-history-graph part is exactly the part it should.

### Stellaris / CK3: event-driven state over full simulation

Paradox grand strategy titles are frequently mistaken for full simulations but are substantially event-driven: a modest set of tracked variables per character/country (opinion, stability, war exhaustion, stress) plus a large content library of triggered events and modifiers that fire against those variables on timers, thresholds, or random rolls. The "simulation" players perceive is mostly the *density and writing quality* of the event content layered on a comparatively simple variable model, not deep agent cognition. This is the same shape as Kenshi's world states and STALKER's shipped faction wars: few live variables, large content table of reactions.

### STALKER: the A-Life ambition-versus-reality gap

STALKER's original pitch and continuing fan mythology is "A-Life": a simulation where hundreds of independent NPCs/mutants live full lives, fight faction wars, and migrate, all offscreen. What actually shipped across the original trilogy was thinner than the marketing, and Clear Sky's faction war was widely considered under-realized, so much so that a fan mod ("The Faction War," https://www.moddb.com/mods/stalker-clear-sky-the-faction-war) got closer to the original vision than the shipped game. STALKER 2 repeated the pattern almost exactly: "A-Life 2.0" was advertised as full offscreen simulation, shipped broken/absent, and was patched in over multiple post-launch updates (faction territory control finally added in a 1.7 patch per https://www.pcgamer.com/games/fps/stalker-2-has-faction-wars-now-enemies-can-expand-control-over-new-territories-in-its-1-7-jumbo-patch-that-also-adds-mega-hard-mode-and-makes-hiding-in-bushes-meaningful/).

The lesson, repeated twice by the same studio: full live agent simulation of "faction wars across the whole map, all the time" is one of the highest-risk features a team can commit to publicly, because it is simultaneously the hardest thing to get right and the thing players most concretely check for by traveling around and looking for it.

### Fallout: New Vegas: the reputation matrix

New Vegas's reputation system tracks two independent, never-decreasing scalars per faction: Fame (positive) and Infamy (negative), each with four thresholds, and your effective standing is a function of both rather than one signed number. This means a history of both massacring and then helping a faction produces a distinct, confused state ("Unpredictable") rather than a full reset, players cannot grind back to a clean slate, only offset. High Fame unlocks discounts, gear, and safehouses; high Infamy triggers hit squads and map-wide hostility. Sources: https://fallout.wiki/wiki/Fallout:_New_Vegas_Reputation, https://blogofarcanesecrets.wordpress.com/2017/09/07/10-good-ideas-fallout-new-vegas-and-the-faction-system/.

This is a genuinely clever, cheap-to-implement design: it needs only two integers and four thresholds per faction, no live agents, and it produces a strong illusion of a faction "remembering everything you did" because nothing is ever silently forgiven.

### RimWorld: the AI storyteller as pacing director

RimWorld's "storyteller" doesn't simulate a world so much as it watches a handful of summary statistics about the colony (population, wealth, recent event history, threat scaling) and picks the next scripted incident from a categorized event database, weighted so the drama arcs rather than spikes randomly. Critically, per Tynan Sylvester's GDC talk (https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1024232/-RimWorld-Contrarian-Ridiculous-and), the storyteller deliberately *eases off* as a colony nears collapse so that a death spiral plays out as a long dramatic arc instead of an abrupt wall, because "drama is good" is a higher priority than "simulation is accurate." Left 4 Dead's AI Director (Mike Booth, GDC 2009) is the FPS-genre ancestor of this idea: it tracks player stress/health and throttles zombie spawns and set-piece "hordes" to keep a hand-shaped tension curve rather than spawning by a fixed schedule or a raw simulation of infected population. Source: https://left4dead.fandom.com/wiki/The_Director.

The lesson directly reusable for Aegis's own pacing-director requirement: a director should consume a *small* number of legible world-state summary stats (not the full graph) and choose from an authored content pool, with an explicit dramaturgical bias (ease off after a big beat, escalate after quiet periods), rather than being a neutral scheduler.

### Wildermyth: storylets and role-casting

Wildermyth authors discrete scenes ("plays") with role slots that have requirement/scoring functions, then casts whichever procedurally generated characters best fit each role at the moment the scene fires, the same logic as casting actors for a play rather than writing bespoke dialogue per character. Campaigns are strung as a sequence of authored "must-happen" beats with proceduraly selected content filling the gaps between them ("string of pearls" structure). Source: https://wildermyth.com/wiki/Story_Inputs_and_Outputs, https://cjleo.com/blog/the-power-of-wildermyths-modular-storytelling-in-game-design/.

This maps directly onto Aegis's already-locked storylet and Wildermyth-style role-casting commitments: the faction sim's job is not to generate prose, it's to generate *facts and role-eligible characters* that storylets can query and cast against.

## Pitfalls and Failure Modes

- **Rich simulation nobody notices.** The single most repeated warning across sources: "anything in the Game Model that doesn't copy into the Player Model is worthless" (Playtank, https://playtank.io/2024/10/12/the-systemic-master-scale/), and hidden complexity that never surfaces in a mental model is indistinguishable from noise (Frictional Games, "The Complexity Fallacy," https://frictionalgames.blogspot.com/2017/06/the-complexity-fallacy.html, citing an actual shipped background trade-route economy that players only ever perceived as random ship encounters). A simulation only earns its dev cost if there's a dedicated narration/UI layer translating its state into player-legible signals (a rumor, a visible caravan, a changed shop stock, a letter, a named NPC's grudge).
- **Endless stalemate wars.** Bannerlord's inert, unwinnable wars show that any live diplomacy/war simulation needs a designed forced-resolution path (timers, forced peace on elimination, vassalization) or it will find a degenerate state that actively damages the "living world" pitch by being visibly, persistently broken.
- **A-Life ambition versus engineering reality.** STALKER shipped a thinner faction-war system than promised, twice, across two different games/engines, because full live agent simulation across an entire map is one of the hardest things to build and debug, and it is exactly the feature players go looking for by wandering the map, so shortfalls are maximally visible.
- **Treadmill fatigue from scripted escalation at scale.** Shadow of War's fortress/Nemesis meta eventually drew criticism for turning into a repetitive capture-point grind at the endgame (compounded by the loot-box controversy around orc "upgrades"), showing that even well-designed scripted escalation needs a finite, curated arc; infinite procedural refill of the same beat shape fatigues players just as fast as an under-simulated world disappoints them.
- **Reputation flattening.** A single global karma/reputation scalar (early Fallout-style) can't represent "hated by the Legion, beloved by the NCR" simultaneously and forces every faction reaction through one number; New Vegas's per-faction, dual-scalar (Fame/Infamy) design fixes this cheaply, but a design that only tracks per-faction standing with no global read has the opposite problem: nothing lets the world react to "this person is a legendary hero/monster" independent of any one faction's opinion.
- **World states nobody can discover.** Kenshi's town-override triggers exist but are undocumented in-game; players learn them from wikis, which is a deliberate discoverability tradeoff (mystery over legibility) that works for Kenshi's genre positioning but would clash with Aegis's quest-traceability requirement (every quest must trace to a world fact) if faction-caused changes aren't exposed anywhere queryable in-fiction.

## Recommendations for Aegis

1. **Build architecture 2 (event-driven world-state faction model) as the backbone, not architecture 1 (full agent simulation).** Track a small per-faction state vector (territory control list, strength/manpower abstraction, treasury/resource abstraction, standing war/peace/feud relations to other factions, a short list of named leader NPCs) and advance it on a coarse tick (say, weekly game-time) via authored transition rules and causal-grammar-generated events, the same shape as Kenshi's world states, CK3's event chains, and DF's world-gen history. This is buildable and debuggable by a solo dev, is fully deterministic, and every transition is a natural, cheap write into the existing world fact/relationship graph, which is the thing quests actually query. Do not build a live economy/logistics simulation (X4-style) or thousands of independently cognitive agents (STALKER A-Life-style): both are documented, repeated failure points even for well-funded studios, and the payoff is disproportionately invisible to players relative to cost (Frictional/Playtank's core warning).

2. **Every faction action must write a fact, and every fact must be narratable, not just loggable.** The recurring failure mode is simulation that never reaches the player. Pair each world-state transition with a mandatory "surfacing" hook: a rumor storylet, a changed map marker/territory color, an NPC dialogue line, a courier letter, or a scene description delta. If a faction event can't be cheaply surfaced through one of these channels, don't model it, the cost isn't justified. This also directly satisfies the locked "every quest must trace to a world fact" requirement: the fact graph becomes the single source both storylets and quest generation query, so there is never a quest that references state the player had no way to perceive.

3. **Split reputation into per-faction standing plus a separate global fame/infamy track**, following New Vegas's model but adding the aggregate axis New Vegas lacks. Per-faction: a simple two-scalar (goodwill earned / harm done) pair per faction, non-resettable, thresholded, driving that faction's prices, access, and hostility, exactly like New Vegas's Fame/Infamy. Global: a separate Renown/Infamy score built from Death's Toll-adjacent milestones (rare feats, notorious kills, resurrection-related events) that other factions can react to on first contact even before they have any direct history with the player, letting a brand-new faction in NG+ world-regen greet a legendary Aegis-bearer appropriately without needing pre-existing per-faction history. This also gives the pacing director and storylet system a cheap global knob to gate high-drama content behind.

4. **Give the pacing director a small, legible input set and an explicit dramaturgical bias, RimWorld/Left 4 Dead style, not a neutral scheduler.** Feed it summary stats only (recent faction-event density, player level/renown, time since last major beat, current act/critical-path position) rather than the raw fact graph, and have it choose from a budgeted pool of authored faction-event storylets per in-game week/region, easing off right after a major escalation and building tension during quiet stretches. This keeps faction activity from either flooding the player with simultaneous feuds or going silent for hours, and keeps the 10-15h critical path from being drowned out or derailed by background faction noise; the director should be able to suppress or defer non-critical faction events near critical-path beats and vice versa.

5. **Reserve Nemesis-style persistent named-NPC memory for a bounded roster, not the whole faction.** Track full grudge/memory state (was defeated by player, escaped and returned scarred, was spared, rose in rank) only for a capped set of "notable" NPCs per faction (leaders and a handful of lieutenants), the same scale Shadow of War actually uses (a fortress has at most 6 warchiefs plus an overlord, not thousands of tracked orcs). This is the cheapest, highest-perceived-value piece of "the world remembers me" and composes naturally with the world fact graph (a notable NPC's memory *is* a set of facts about them) and with Wildermyth-style role-casting (notable NPCs are exactly the pool storylets cast into recurring roles).

6. **Design an explicit forced-resolution path for every faction conflict state, before shipping any war/feud system.** Bannerlord's stalemate failure is entirely a missing-exit-condition bug, not an inherent flaw of simulated war. Any faction-vs-faction war or feud state in Aegis should carry a maximum duration or an escalating pressure toward one of: negotiated peace, one side's territory/resource collapse triggering forced capitulation, or player-triggered resolution via a quest. This is cheap to add up front (a counter and a threshold) and expensive to retrofit after players start reporting "these two factions have been at war for 40 hours and nothing happens."
