# Single-Player RPG Economy Design: Making Money Matter for a Whole Playthrough

## Overview

Almost every open-world RPG follows the same arc: money is tight and meaningful in the first few hours, then somewhere in the middle third it stops mattering because income (loot sold, quest rewards, dungeon clears) grows geometrically while player-facing prices grow linearly or not at all. Once that crossover happens, the economy stops being a system the player reasons about and becomes a number that goes up. Shamus Young's analysis of Skyrim frames this as not really a bug but an inevitable consequence of a loop players like: kill well-equipped enemies, loot their gear, sell it for a fair price. Vendor gold caps are the one lever Bethesda pulled, and Young's verdict is that this "encourages tedious fast travel between merchants to offload goods, making the game less fun without really making it more balanced" (Shamus Young, "Who Broke the In-Game Economy?", https://www.shamusyoung.com/twentysidedtale/?p=48598). The core math failure: enemies infinitely respawn with sellable gear, and end-game item prices scale far slower than end-game gold income, so the two curves diverge permanently.

The fix is never "reduce loot," it's making the sink side of the ledger scale as aggressively as the faucet side, and making sinks feel aspirational (things the player wants to spend on) rather than taxes (things that just delete currency to keep a spreadsheet balanced). Aegis's design already separates coin from Essence (Essence buys attributes/growth, coin buys the material world), which is a structural advantage most of these case studies didn't have: it means coin never has to double as the leveling currency, so it can be dedicated entirely to a stay-relevant sink economy without XP pressure warping prices.

Standard economic-design vocabulary applies directly: every game economy is a set of **faucets** (mechanisms that create currency: loot, quest rewards, selling goods) and **drains/sinks** (mechanisms that destroy or absorb it: repairs, tolls, training). Raph Koster's SWG-era framing: "a spigot of cash coming into the game, a big ol' sink where the money sloshes around, and a set of drains where the money goes out the bottom" (via discussion of Koster's virtual economy essays, https://www.raphkoster.com/tag/ian-schreiber/). "If you do not enforce ongoing expenditures (drains), you will have infinite accumulation of wealth and unbalance in the game design and poor game longevity" (summarized from Koster/Schreiber economy design literature, same source). EVE Online's monthly economic reports are the industry's most public long-running case study in tuning this balance at scale, explicitly tracking ISK faucets against ISK sinks and destruction events (https://www.eveonline.com/news/view/monthly-economic-report-october-2025, https://tagn.wordpress.com/category/eve-online/eve-monthly-economic-report/).

## How Notable Games Solved (or Failed to Solve) This

### Skyrim: the finite-vendor-gold band-aid

Each merchant has a gold pool (roughly 750-4000 depending on type/perks) that regenerates daily. The intent is scarcity: you can't dump unlimited loot on one vendor. In practice, with enough merchants and fast travel, the cap barely slows a determined seller, it just adds tedium (Young, op. cit.). The deeper failure is structural: nothing in the world consumes coin at a rate that scales with player power. Repairs don't exist (removed from Oblivion's model), there's no upkeep, and property (houses) is a flat one-time purchase with no ongoing cost. Once smithing/enchanting let players craft gear that outclasses anything for sale, the vendor side of the economy becomes irrelevant to a player's actual gear progression, coin only matters for a few big one-off purchases (houses, spouse, some training), after which it's pure surplus.

### The Witcher 3: crafting materials as the real sink, but under-tuned

Coin flows in fast from monster contracts, treasure hunts, and loot-selling. The intended sink is crafting: making Witcher gear (and later Grandmaster/Blood and Wine gear) consumes both money and reagents, and getting materials at volume requires selling excess crafting components. Community post-mortems (HN thread on "How the Witcher 3's economy was saved by polynomial least squares," https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=10004443; forum analysis at https://rpgwatch.com/forum/threads/witcher-3-designing-a-crafting-economy.34492/) describe an economy that felt "mostly broken" for the same underlying reason as Skyrim: side-quest and loot income outran crafting costs once a player had a few hours of side content banked, so by mid-game gold stopped being a real constraint even though crafting materials briefly were. Blood and Wine tried to re-tension this by raising both prices and material costs for Grandmaster gear, but by then players were already sitting on surplus wealth from the base game, so the new expensive tier read as a fresh grind bolted onto an already-solved economy rather than a natural next step.

### Kingdom Come: Deliverance: the most grounded attempt, and where it still leaks

KCD is the strongest source of *positive* patterns for Aegis because its tone and structure are closest to ours:
- **Haggling with reputation gating.** Prices aren't fixed: you negotiate via a haggling minigame, but your success ceiling is capped by reputation with that merchant/region. New or disreputable characters get lowballed on sales and overcharged on purchases; repeated failed haggling burns down merchant patience and can lock you out of negotiating at all for a time (https://www.thegamer.com/kingdom-come-deliverance-2-haggling-best-prices-how-to-get/, https://gamerant.com/kingdom-come-deliverance-2-kcd2-how-get-better-prices-haggle/). This makes reputation a genuine economic stat, not just a flavor number, and it gives town life (building standing with a merchant/settlement) a direct coin payoff.
- **Tiered, skill-gated repair.** Repair cost scales with the gear's own quality/value (better armor costs more to fix), and a Maintenance skill lets a player eventually self-repair with owned kits (Armorer's, Blacksmith's, Tailor's), trading coin sink for time/skill investment (https://segmentnext.com/kingdom-come-deliverance-repairing-weapons-and-armor/, https://deltiasgaming.com/how-to-repair-in-kingdom-come-deliverance-2/). This is a sink that scales with wealth tier automatically: richer gear costs more to maintain, so upkeep tracks the player's own power curve without needing separate tuning.
- **Where it broke down**: community consensus and the existence of "Tougher Economy" / "Economy Improved" overhaul mods (https://www.nexusmods.com/kingdomcomedeliverance2/mods/281, https://www.nexusmods.com/kingdomcomedeliverance2/mods/3004) point at the same root problem as the others: "the game doesn't really have money sinks" at endgame scale. Repair and haggling are real but small relative to the coin available from quests, chests, and combat loot by the back third of the game, so players still end up sitting on thousands of groschen with nothing to do with it. The lesson: grounded, diegetic sinks (haggling, per-item repair) are the right texture, but they must be sized to consume a meaningful fraction of *mid-and-late* income, not just early income.

### Elden Ring: unifying currency and XP, and why that's the opposite of Aegis's problem

Runes are simultaneously the leveling currency (spent at Sites of Grace to raise stats) and the shop currency (spent with merchants and at Twin Maiden Husks for spells, ashes, upgrade materials). This single-resource design creates constant tension: every rune is a choice between "level up now" and "buy something now," and death makes runes recoverable-but-risked, which keeps them meaningfully scarce all game because there is only one pool to spend from and leveling costs scale up continuously (fextralife rune guide: https://eldenring.wiki.fextralife.com/Runes; discussion of the dual-purpose design: https://www.oreateai.com/blog/understanding-runes-the-currency-of-elden-ring/09ca934e775955d98d61d6f6bf6670c7). Souls-likes never suffer Skyrim's problem because there's no way to "finish" spending, the level-up sink is bottomless and always the best use of currency.

**This is directly relevant to Aegis by contrast, not by imitation.** Aegis deliberately separates Essence (bought with deeds, spent on attributes) from coin. That means coin loses Elden Ring's built-in bottomless sink, once a player hits a comfortable attribute/gear baseline, coin has nothing structural pulling it out of their pocket unless we build that pull by hand. The upside: coin can be a pure "material world" resource, its meaning doesn't get diluted by leveling math, and we can design coin sinks that are about *lifestyle and capability* (a smithy, a crew, a safehouse) rather than being reskinned XP. But it means we cannot lean on "coin is also how you get stronger" to keep it relevant; Aegis's sinks have to do that whole job themselves, at every wealth tier, for 30+ hours.

### Vendor economies with structure: finite gold, regional prices, arbitrage

Skyrim's finite-vendor-gold is a weak, single-city version of a much better pattern: Mount & Blade's regional/simulated markets. Bannerlord's economy runs on real supply and demand: each settlement produces specific goods based on geography and culture (coastal towns produce fish cheaply, mountain towns produce iron and precious metals), and prices shift with wars, banditry, and seasonal supply, so "furs will be difficult to find in the south and will sell for an above-average price in southern settlements, while dates are only sold in the south... purchasing them there and selling them up north will return a healthy profit" (https://www.gamerguides.com/mount-and-blade-ii-bannerlord/guide/economy/how-to-make-money/trading-for-a-profit, https://www.shokuho.org/blog/bannerlord-economy-guide.html, https://mountandblade.fandom.com/wiki/Economy). Caravans let a player convert coin into a passive income stream by capitalizing a trade route rather than personally running it, coin becomes productive capital, not just banked wealth. This is the single best model in this research set for "what does money let a wealthy player *do*" beyond buying gear: it becomes an investment.

### Crafting economies going obsolete: the structural reasons, and the fix

Community design discussion (MMORPG.com forum thread "Why aren't crafted items better?", https://forums.mmorpg.com/discussion/398885/why-arent-crafted-items-better) converges on why crafted gear reliably loses relevance:
1. Best-in-slot gear is deliberately placed on raid/boss/quest rewards because "adventure and its reward" is the primary loop developers want to protect, crafting that outclasses that reward undercuts the intended reward loop.
2. Without item degradation or gear that leaves the economy (breaks, gets bound, gets consumed), there's no ongoing demand for replacement crafted items, once everyone has a set, the crafting economy has no repeat customers.
3. The forum consensus fix is diversifying what crafting is *for*: consumables (not just permanent upgrades), augment/socket slots that only crafters can fill on top-tier drop gear, and cosmetic/utility crafting, so crafting stays relevant by being complementary to the loot loop rather than competing with it for the "best gear" slot.

This maps well onto Aegis: crafted gear shouldn't chase parity with authored/quest gear forever, it should own categories quest/loot gear doesn't: consumables (potions, poisons, field rations), upgrade materials that make existing gear better (sockets, reinforcement), and regionally-flavored or build-specific gear that fills gaps rather than topping charts.

## Pitfalls / Failure Modes (Synthesis)

1. **Geometric income vs. linear (or flat) sinks.** The single most common failure. Enemy loot value and quest rewards tend to scale with player level/power; sinks (property, one-time purchases) usually don't. The gap compounds every hour played.
2. **Sinks that are one-time, not recurring.** A house you buy once, a horse you buy once: real, but they cap out. Once bought, they stop pulling coin. Recurring sinks (upkeep, wages, tolls, spoilage) are what keep draining a growing income stream.
3. **Vendor caps as the only lever.** Skyrim shows a finite-gold vendor cap alone just adds travel-tedium, it doesn't create a reason to spend, only friction against selling. Caps work as a *complement* to real sinks (they push players toward regional arbitrage and haggling), not as a substitute for sinks.
4. **Sinks that read as taxes.** A flat "fast travel costs gold" fee or an arbitrary tariff feels punitive because it buys nothing the player wants, it just makes an action they were already going to take cost more. Aspirational sinks (a manor, a warhorse, a master's tutelage, grandmaster-tier crafting reagents) work because the player is choosing to spend toward something they want, the coin leaves willingly.
5. **Crafting racing to obsolescence.** If crafted gear's whole value proposition is "better stats than loot," it will eventually either trivialize the loot game (Skyrim's smithing/enchanting) or get patched/power-crept into irrelevance (Witcher 3's Grandmaster tier arriving too late to matter economically). Crafting needs its own lane (consumables, augments, regional/flavor items) rather than a stat-race with drops.
6. **No mechanism connecting price to world state.** Static, hand-tuned prices can't express "there's a war on, bread costs triple in the siege town." Static economies also can't create arbitrage-driven play (Mount & Blade's regional specialization is the counterexample); a world with only one price for iron everywhere has no room for a trade-goods playstyle at all.
7. **NG+ wealth carryover breaking the next loop's tension immediately.** Games that let currency carry into a New Game Plus (Like a Dragon: Infinite Wealth carries all money and equipment forward, https://www.rpgsite.net/guide/15397-like-dragon-infinite-wealth-new-game-plus-explained-now-to-unlock-what-carries-over-t-ng) solve the "did my progress matter" problem but reopen the "does money still matter" problem on day one of the new cycle, unless the new cycle also raises its price ceiling to match.

## Recommendations for Aegis

These are written to be consistent with the locked decisions: coin is separate from Essence, seven-attribute classless system, 30h+ authored critical path plus open activity breadth, procedurally-generated world facts, NG+ with full world regeneration and carried character, four must-have activity families (crafting, wilderness, crime, town life) each needing to both generate and consume money.

**1. Give coin a bottomless, aspirational sink ladder instead of relying on Essence's leveling pull.** Because Essence (not coin) buys power, coin needs its own reason to always be wanted. Build a tiered ladder of standing sinks that never caps: property (a first room, then a house, then a hall, then a keep, each with rising purchase price *and* rising upkeep), a retinue (hirelings, apprentices, a caravan crew, each drawing wages), and a master-tier crafting/training ceiling that keeps rising (grandmaster reagents, rare tutors, guild dues) as the player's own skill/attribute ceiling rises. The point is that at every wealth tier there's a next rung visibly priced above the player's current means, so coin is always "for" something concrete, never just banked.

**2. Make repair/upkeep scale with the player's own gear value, not a flat rate, and gate self-sufficiency behind a skill (KCD's model, done more aggressively).** Repair/maintenance cost as a percentage of item value means richer gear automatically taxes richer players more, no manual re-tuning needed as power grows. Let a Maintenance-like skill or crafting trade let players self-repair with owned materials/tools, trading coin for time and skill investment, this rewards the crafting activity family with a real, recurring reason to exist (players who invest in smithing/tailoring save real coin over a 30h run) without ever making self-repair fully free (materials still cost coin or foraging effort).

**3. Tie regional prices and scarcity directly to the world fact graph, and let trade-goods arbitrage be a first-class wilderness/town activity.** Since Aegis already generates world facts causally (war, famine, trade disputes, monster incursions), wire prices to those facts at the region level: a war-zone settlement should show inflated food/medicine prices and depressed luxury-goods prices; an isolated or embargoed settlement should show the Bannerlord-style arbitrage opportunity (buy low where a good is abundant, haul it, sell high where a war fact or trade-disruption fact has made it scarce). This gives coin generation a genuine skill-and-knowledge component (a player who tracks the world's fact graph makes better trade decisions) rather than a flat "sell loot for gold" faucet, and it makes town life and crime (smuggling embargoed goods, black-market fencing in a warzone) mechanically distinct rather than reskins of each other.

**4. Keep crafted gear out of the stat-race with quest/loot gear; give it exclusive lanes instead.** Crafting should own: consumables (which are inherently self-consuming sinks, i.e. always need re-buying/re-crafting materials), augmentation of existing gear (sockets, reinforcement, reforging) rather than replacement of it, and regionally- or build-flavored items that fill gaps authored loot doesn't cover. This avoids the Witcher 3/Skyrim failure mode where crafting either trivializes the loot game or gets orphaned by a late, over-tuned top tier, and it means crafting materials (gathered via wilderness activity) stay in perpetual demand across the whole 30h run rather than only mattering during a single gear-up phase.

**5. Use haggling and reputation, not just vendor gold caps, as the primary vendor-side friction, and let reputation be a legible cross-activity currency.** Adopt KCD's pattern directly: negotiated prices with a reputation ceiling per merchant/settlement, so town-life investment (standing, favors, repeat business) pays off in hard coin terms, not just dialogue flavor. Combine with modest per-vendor liquidity (not Skyrim's flat caps, but liquidity that itself reflects the region's world-fact-driven wealth) so large windfalls (a big dungeon haul, a caravan's cargo) naturally have to be distributed across multiple markets or converted through a middleman (at a cut, feeding the crime activity family: fences who pay less but ask no questions).

**6. For NG+, regenerate prices and the sink ladder alongside the world, and let World Hostility Tiers explicitly raise the coin cost of staying geared.** Since the character and wealth carry across NG+ but the world fully regenerates, the new cycle's price ceiling must regenerate upward too or the carried wealth trivializes day one (the Infinite Wealth problem). Concretely: tie top-of-ladder sink prices (grandmaster crafting, keep upkeep, elite hirelings, covenant-gated services) to the active World Hostility Tier and any stacked covenants, so a harsher NG+ world is also a harsher economy: rarer high-tier materials, higher upkeep in a more dangerous world, pricier mercenaries. This reuses the same tier system already locked in for enemy/hazard generation as the economy's re-tensioning knob, so wealthy NG+ characters face a real fresh challenge without a separate wealth-reset mechanic (which would feel like a punishment rather than a natural consequence of choosing a harder world).
