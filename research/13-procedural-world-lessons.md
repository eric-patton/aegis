# Procedural World Games in General: Lessons Learned, Great Patterns, Things to Avoid

## Overview

This file is the wide-angle survey the other research files deliberately are not. Where [01-procedural-world-plus-authored-narrative.md](01-procedural-world-plus-authored-narrative.md) goes deep on procgen-plus-narrative (Dwarf Fortress, Caves of Qud, Wildermyth, Nemesis, storylets, Skyrim, Kenshi), [06-quest-and-dialogue-generation.md](06-quest-and-dialogue-generation.md) on quest and dialogue generation, [11-daggerfall-scale.md](11-daggerfall-scale.md) on Daggerfall's scale problem, and [12-kdm.md](12-kdm.md) on Kingdom Death, this one asks: across the whole genre of procedural world games, from Elite (1984) to No Man's Sky, from Minecraft to Brogue, what does the craft community actually agree it has learned? The answer is remarkably consistent, and it converges hard on Aegis's existing bets while flagging a few risks specific to Aegis's shape (text presentation, infinite NG+, a fact graph as the load-bearing structure).

One framing carries the whole file: **generation is a multiplier on meaning, never a source of it.** Every celebrated success (Spelunky, Minecraft, RimWorld, Qud) puts human authorship somewhere structural (templates, rules, pacing, grammar, curation) and lets the generator multiply it. Every famous failure (No Man's Sky at launch, Elite Dangerous's ambient emptiness, Daggerfall's dungeons per file 11) multiplied something too thin to be worth multiplying. Aegis's TUI presentation makes this sharper, not softer: text costs nothing to generate at scale, so the entire burden lands on whether the generated thing *means* anything, and templated prose is easier to catch than templated geometry. That cuts both ways and gets its own section below.

## Craft lore: the theory the practitioners actually use

### The 10,000 Bowls of Oatmeal problem (Kate Compton)

The single most-cited idea in procgen craft writing. From Compton's essay ["So you want to build a generator"](https://galaxykate0.tumblr.com/post/139774965871/so-you-want-to-build-a-generator): "I can easily generate 10,000 bowls of plain oatmeal, with each oat being in a different position and different orientation, and mathematically speaking they will all be completely unique. But the user will likely just see a lot of oatmeal. **Perceptual uniqueness is the real metric,** and it's darn tough." Her practical decomposition:

- **Perceptual differentiation** (this one is not identical to the last one) is often sufficient for background content: trees, terrain, filler NPCs. It's cheap.
- **Perceptual uniqueness** (this one is memorable in itself) is what quests, named NPCs, relics, and anything the player is asked to care about must clear. It's expensive and generators mostly fail at it.
- Her rule-of-thumb test: "Would you write fanfic about this generated object?" If no player would ever retell it, it's oatmeal (also covered in her [Game Developer piece "Practical procedural generation for everyone"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/practical-procedural-generation-for-everyone-)).

Emily Short's follow-up, ["Bowls of Oatmeal and Text Generation"](https://emshort.blog/2016/09/21/bowls-of-oatmeal-and-text-generation/), applies this directly to prose: generated text variation that doesn't carry *information the player can act on or care about* is oatmeal regardless of how large the grammar is. This is the single most important essay for Aegis specifically, because Aegis's surface is almost entirely text.

### Evaluating generators: expressive range and the generative space

[Gillian Smith and Jim Whitehead's "Analyzing the expressive range of a level generator" (2010)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228411962_Analyzing_the_expressive_range_of_a_level_generator) established the standard evaluation method: generate thousands of artifacts, score each on two or more designer-meaningful metrics (for Aegis: faction count entangled per settlement, war depth per history, relic-to-event linkage density), and plot the distribution as a heatmap. What it reveals, reliably, is that generators cluster: the "infinite variety" generator actually produces 90% of its output in a small blob of the possibility space, with unreachable holes the designer assumed were covered. The follow-on literature ([metric selection improvements, 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02366)) refines the metrics but keeps the method. The lesson is operational: **build the histogram tooling early and run it in CI**, exactly parallel to Aegis's existing journey/sim verification philosophy. Compton and Mateas's ["Casual Creators" paper](https://computationalcreativity.net/iccc2015/proceedings/10_2Compton.pdf) adds the complementary frame of a generator as a designed *possibility space* the designer sculpts, not a machine that emits products.

Wave Function Collapse and its ancestor model synthesis ([Boris the Brave's explainer](https://www.boristhebrave.com/2020/04/13/wave-function-collapse-explained/), [Wikipedia on model synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_synthesis)) matter here mostly as a proof point: constraint-propagation over hand-authored adjacency examples (used in Bad North, Townscaper, and Caves of Qud itself) is the tile-level version of the same principle Aegis's causal grammar applies at the history level. Local choices constrained to stay globally coherent read as designed; unconstrained choices read as noise.

### The roguelike tradition: hundreds of hours against one generator

The [Berlin Interpretation (2008)](https://www.roguebasin.com/index.php/Berlin_Interpretation) canonized "random environment generation to increase replayability" as the genre's first high-value factor, and the genre is the longest-running experiment in what happens when players spend hundreds of hours against a single generator. The consistent findings (this paragraph is community craft lore, well-attested across developer talks and forums rather than a single citable study):

- **Players learn the grammar, always.** After enough runs a Spelunky or Slay the Spire player perceives the templates, the item pools, and the odds directly. This is not a failure; it *becomes the game*. Mastery of the generator's distribution is the late-game content (Slay the Spire's Ascension climb and daily runs, Spelunky's daily challenge, both explicitly built on seeded runs so the community can share one instance of the generator's output as a common text).
- What keeps a learned generator fresh is **growing the pools and interaction rules**, not widening the randomness: new items, enemies, and modifiers that recombine with everything already learned. This maps exactly onto Aegis's file-04 conclusion that NG+ tiers must unlock new content, not scale numbers, and onto the storylet architecture's additive-content property from file 01.
- **Content versus noise**: the community distinction between a generated thing the player must *read and answer* (a Brogue level layout, a shop offering) and a generated thing that merely varies (wall textures, name syllables). Generators earn their keep only on the first kind.

## Games survey

Grouped by what each teaches. Concise per game; depth on adjacent topics lives in files 01, 06, 11, and 12.

### The cautionary tales at scale: No Man's Sky and Elite Dangerous

**No Man's Sky (2016)** is the genre's canonical failure-then-redemption arc. The launch pitch was 18 quintillion procedurally generated planets; the launch reception was that they were, in Vice's exact framing, ["18 quintillion bowls of oatmeal"](https://www.vice.com/en/article/nz7d8q/no-mans-sky-review): mathematically unique, perceptually interchangeable, with nothing to do on any of them that mattered on the next one. "Wide as an ocean, deep as a puddle" became the standard community verdict ([retrospective](https://gideonsgaming.com/no-mans-sky-in-2025-deep-dive-review/)). The instructive part is *what the recovery consisted of*. Across 45+ free major updates ([release log](https://www.nomanssky.com/release-log/), [redemption overview](https://www.club386.com/no-mans-sky-hello-games-redemption-story/)), Hello Games did not fix the generator's variety; they added **meaning on top of it**: an authored story arc (Atlas Rises), base building and settlements players govern ([Frontiers](https://www.nomanssky.com/frontiers-update/)), hand-crafted community Expeditions with over 100 hand-authored milestone rewards ([Expeditions](https://www.nomanssky.com/expeditions-update/)), reworked missions that require actual play instead of marker-following, and multiplayer so discoveries have witnesses. Every single fix is a fix to *stakes and authorship*, not to the noise function. The planets are still oatmeal; the game around them stopped being about the planets.

**Elite Dangerous** re-created a 1:1 Milky Way (400 billion systems, the "Stellar Forge", [technical overview](https://ctrl500.com/tech/how-frontier-managed-to-re-create-our-entire-galaxy-in-elite-dangerous/)) and earned the same complaint in the same words: ["a mile wide but an inch deep"](https://forums.frontier.co.uk/threads/a-mile-wide-but-an-inch-deep.473227/), repetition, "no mysteries to solve or breadcrumbs to follow, just meaningless grind." Notably, its actual gameplay content is hand-placed tokens sprinkled over the generated volume; the generation produces the *space between* content, and players feel that gap directly. Both games prove the same theorem from opposite directions: **scale is not content, and players convert unclaimed scale into an accusation.** Aegis's coarse walkable overworld is already the right call here (density of meaning per traversal-minute, not per square kilometer); file 11's Daggerfall analysis reaches the identical conclusion from 1996.

### The honorable ancestor: Elite (1984)

Elite's galaxy generation is the founding story of seeded worlds and worth knowing precisely. Bell and Braben generated 8 galaxies of 256 systems each from a six-byte seed run through a modified Fibonacci sequence, deriving every system's position, economy, government, and name from the stream, all inside roughly 22K of usable RAM ([The Digital Antiquarian](https://www.filfre.net/2013/12/elite/), [GameSpot's history](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/how-elite-took-flight/1100-6301740/), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elite_(video_game))). Two lessons still current:

1. **Generate-then-curate is as old as the technique itself.** Bell and Braben ran a "beauty parade" of hundreds of candidate universes from different seeds and culled the ones with bad distributions before shipping one. The generator ran at dev time; a human picked the output. (They also famously rejected a candidate galaxy over a rude planet name, a story widely retold in interviews; treat the specific anecdote as folklore, the beauty-parade process as sourced.)
2. **The seed as the world's identity.** Elite's universe was famous and shared: every player's Lave was the same Lave, because the seed shipped with the game. Aegis inverts this (every player's world is different) but the hierarchical-seed architecture from file 05 is a direct descendant, and NG+ makes each seed a bounded "edition" of the world the way Elite's galaxy 1 was.

### Substrates players write on: Minecraft, Valheim, Terraria

**Minecraft** is the strongest counterexample to "procedural worlds can't be loved," and it is essential to be precise about *why* it works, because most of the reasons do not transfer automatically. Its terrain ([Alan Zucconi's deep dive](https://www.alanzucconi.com/2022/06/05/minecraft-world-generation/), [Minecraft Wiki](https://minecraft.wiki/w/World_generation)) layers multiple octaves of gradient noise plus biome parameters (temperature, humidity, erosion, "weirdness") so terrain has structure at every scale, the way real geology does. But the meaning layer is almost entirely **player-authored**: the world becomes memorable because the player builds a house, carves a path, names a mountain. The generated substrate's job is to be (a) locally distinctive enough to navigate by (this valley, that lava fall), and (b) worth modifying. Generated **structures** (villages, strongholds, temples) function as anchors and destinations, and they are the weakest, most oatmeal part of the game: every village blacksmith is the same blacksmith, which players tolerate because villages are resource nodes, not narrative. Aegis takes the opposite deal (the world means things *before* the player arrives), but Minecraft's transferable lesson stands: **give players a way to leave persistent marks** (a cleared dungeon stays cleared, a saved village remembers, a grave marks a death site), because marks convert generated terrain into *their* terrain. Aegis's delta-over-seed save model is exactly the right substrate for this.

Minecraft also supplies the genre's best-documented **versioning cautionary tale**: because terrain regenerates from seed plus algorithm, every algorithm change fractures old worlds at chunk borders, producing sheer cliffs where old and new generation meet ([Tutorial: Updating old terrain](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Tutorial:Updating_old_terrain)); the 1.18 overhaul needed dedicated blending machinery, and community tools like mcmerge exist solely to stitch the seams. For Aegis, whose saves are seed plus delta by design, the implication is hard: **the generator version is part of the save format.** Either regeneration must be bit-stable per world version (freeze the generator per world, ship old generators alongside new ones), or every generator change is a save-breaking change. The cheapest policy given infinite NG+: a world keeps the generator version it was born under for its whole life, and upgrades apply only to freshly generated NG+ worlds. That turns the versioning problem into a feature (each NG+ world can use the newest generator).

**Valheim** demonstrates biome-gated progression on a procedural map ([progression overview](https://www.bamboogaming.net/valheim/progression), [worldgen overview](https://cybrancee.com/blog/how-does-valheim-world-generation-work/)): biomes are laid out roughly by distance from spawn, each biome holds a boss, each boss drops the key item that makes the next biome survivable (Meadows, Black Forest, Swamp, Mountains, Plains, and onward). The map is random but the *difficulty topology* is authored, so the designer keeps control of pacing without controlling the map. Terraria does the same thing in time rather than space (hardmode transforms the world mid-game). Community friction is instructive too: players who wander into the wrong biome early get destroyed, and the game deliberately lets them, using lethality itself as the signpost ([player discussions](https://steamcommunity.com/app/892970/discussions/0/3101267090239222537/)). This is precisely Aegis's World Hostility Tier concept (file 04) applied *within* one world: the fact graph should generate a danger gradient with legible in-fiction signals (refugees, ruins, prices, rumors warning about the region), so exploration order is player-chosen but consequence-signaled.

### Levels that feel designed: Spelunky and Brogue

**Spelunky** is the craft-canonical answer to "how do generated levels feel hand-made" ([Derek Yu's Boss Fight book](https://bossfightbooks.com/products/spelunky-by-derek-yu), [level-generation breakdown](https://procedural-content-generation.fandom.com/wiki/Spelunky), [GMTK video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Uqk5Zf0tw3o)). The generator is tiny: a 4x4 grid of rooms, a guaranteed walkable path from entrance to exit carved first (no bombs or rope required to finish, ever), rooms drawn from small hand-authored template sets keyed to their role on the path (drop rooms, landing rooms, corridor rooms), and hand-authored "chunk" mutations inside templates. Almost everything the player perceives as design is the *templates*, human-authored; the generator only arranges them and guarantees solvability. The proportions are the lesson: **authorship in the parts, generation in the arrangement, and a hard structural guarantee (solvability) enforced by construction rather than by post-hoc testing.** Aegis's analogue: every generated quest chain gets a guaranteed-completable spine by construction (the target exists, is reachable, and cannot be deleted by the coarse tick while the quest is live), with variation composed around that spine from authored parts.

**Brogue** shows the same discipline with a different accent ([Brian Walker's Roguelike Celebration talk on room accretion and "machines"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Uo9-IcHhq_w), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brogue_(video_game))). Levels grow by room accretion, then "machines" (authored multi-room set pieces: a vault with a guardian, a lever puzzle, a flooded chamber) are stamped into the layout, re-running generation locally so the set piece integrates instead of sitting on top. Brogue's reputation for levels that read as deliberate tactical puzzles comes from those authored machines plus aggressively minimal content (few item and monster types, each deeply interactive), the opposite of the more-content-more-variety instinct. For Aegis: a small library of authored "situation machines" (an ambush geometry, a shrine with a cost, a siege in progress) stamped into generated sites will do more than doubling the room-shape vocabulary, and a small, deeply cross-referenced fact vocabulary beats a huge shallow one.

### Simulation as storyteller: RimWorld, Dwarf Fortress's next act, and the fixed-world counterpoint

**RimWorld**'s pacing director is covered for quest purposes in file 06; the genre-level point is Tynan Sylvester's framing itself. He calls RimWorld "a story generator, not a game" and designed the AI Storyteller (Cassandra Classic's tension curves, Phoebe's mercy, Randy's chaos) as an explicit dramatic pacing layer *above* the simulation, deciding when the simulation gets to hurt you ([GDC 2017 talk](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1024232/-RimWorld-Contrarian-Ridiculous-and), [design analysis](https://zaydqazi.substack.com/p/the-story-generator-a-game-design), his book *Designing Games*). Two under-appreciated corollaries from his talks and writing: colonists are deliberately *shallow* simulations wearing legible trait labels, because players' story-projection does the deep work (the system needs to be readable more than it needs to be smart); and the storyteller exists because raw simulation output has terrible dramatic pacing (long boredom, unfair spikes). Aegis's coarse world tick is a simulation; the lesson is that **something must sit above it with editorial authority over when its outputs reach the player**, which in Aegis's architecture is the storylet selection layer acting as dungeon master: choosing which true facts from the graph surface now, at what intensity, on a tension curve.

**Dwarf Fortress** beyond file 01: Tarn Adams's forward-looking talks (the GDC 2016 myth-generation demo, [PC Gamer on the magic-system plans](https://www.pcgamer.com/dwarf-fortress-creator-tarn-adams-talks-about-simulating-the-most-complex-magic-system-ever/), the Villains release work in [his Game Developer Q&A](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/q-a-dissecting-the-development-of-i-dwarf-fortress-i-with-creator-tarn-adams)) all push one direction: generating not just the world's *events* but its *rules* (each world's own creation myth, from which that world's magic, afterlife, and cosmology derive), and giving historical actors ongoing off-screen schemes (villains with plots, networks, and interrogatable motives). The transferable idea for Aegis, at drastically smaller scope: a few **world-scoped generated rules** per NG+ world (what this world's curses actually do, what this world's dominant faith holds, one cosmological quirk), derived from the same seed tree and referenced by the fact graph, make each NG+ world differ in *kind* rather than only in map and names. That is a direct answer to the learned-generator problem: pool growth at the rules level.

**The fixed-world counterpoint: Mount & Blade (and Kenshi, per file 01).** Mount & Blade's Calradia is entirely hand-authored (one map, ~50 towns and castles, 100+ villages, [world overview](https://mountandblade.fandom.com/wiki/Calradia)) with heavy systemic simulation running on top: lords wage wars, borders redraw every few weeks, a simulated feudal economy moves prices with supply and demand. It delivers "no two campaigns alike" with zero worldgen, because the *simulation state* varies, not the geography, and it gains something generation cannot buy: every player knows Calradia, so community knowledge, mods, and sequel continuity accrete on a shared world. Kenshi (file 01) makes the same trade. This is the strongest argument *against* Aegis's worldgen bet, so it deserves a straight answer: Aegis generates anyway because infinite NG+ requires genuinely fresh worlds (a reset fixed map is a rerun, and file 04 rules that out) and because a fixed world's authored density cannot be re-earned each cycle. But the counterpoint teaches two things worth taking. First, most of the *felt* variety in M&B and Kenshi comes from the simulation, not the map: Aegis's coarse tick and faction dynamics will carry more of the "no two playthroughs" load than the worldgen will, so they deserve at least equal investment. Second, shared reference points have real value: Aegis's fixed anchors per world (file 01's Joppa pattern) and any cross-world constants (the same pantheon skeleton, a recurring order or artifact lineage across NG+ crossings) are how Aegis buys back a little of Calradia's "everyone knows this place" without giving up fresh worlds.

## The text-presentation angle: Aegis's cheat and Aegis's trap

Most of the genre's famous failures are partly *asset* failures: No Man's Sky's oatmeal is oatmeal because there are finitely many creature parts, rock shaders, and building kits, and 3D asset variety is brutally expensive. Aegis dodges this entirely. Text has no asset pipeline; a fact graph entry costs bytes; a thousand distinct settlements cost the same to render as ten. This is a genuine structural advantage and the reason a two-order-of-magnitude-smaller team can attempt a Qud-class world at all.

But text moves the failure point, it does not remove it, and it makes the remaining failure *more* visible:

- **Mad-libs are naked in prose.** A player may not notice two procedurally assembled ruins sharing a wall kit; every player notices "The {faction} of {town} seeks the {relic}" the second time the sentence shape repeats. Prose templates are read closely, sequentially, and consciously in a way 3D scenery is not. Emily Short's oatmeal essay (above) is the definitive statement: surface variation of synonyms does not help, only variation in *information content* does. The mitigation is architectural, not lexical: many small composable prose fragments keyed to facts, assembled per-scene, with the same fact rendered differently by different speakers and surfaces (rumor, ledger, epitaph, drunk), so repetition of *structure* is masked by genuine variation in *perspective and content*. Never one big template per content type.
- **Text raises the fanfic bar.** In a 3D game a striking vista can be memorable without meaning anything. Text has no vistas; the only thing Aegis can be memorable *with* is specificity: names, motives, consequences, callbacks. Compton's fanfic test is close to a literal acceptance criterion for Aegis's generated content: if a generated NPC, relic, or war would not support one sentence of player retelling ("the ferryman who bought back his daughter's debt with my sword"), it should be background texture and must not be offered as content.
- **Text is mercilessly diffable.** Players screenshot and compare prose. Two players (or one player across NG+ worlds, which is the dangerous case: the same player sees many worlds by design) will spot shared sentence skeletons instantly. The learned-generator problem arrives *faster* in text, so prose-fragment pools need the same growth-per-tier treatment as enemy pools.
- The compensating gift: **text is the native format of history.** Chronicles, letters, ledgers, epitaphs, songs are all text, so Aegis's fact graph can surface itself in-fiction with zero translation loss, something no 3D game manages (Dwarf Fortress needed a separate Legends *mode*; Aegis can put legends in the world's own books).

## Top lessons learned

1. **Perceptual uniqueness is the metric; mathematical uniqueness is worthless.** ([Compton](https://galaxykate0.tumblr.com/post/139774965871/so-you-want-to-build-a-generator), NMS launch.) For Aegis: measure and review generated worlds by what a player could *retell*, not by entity counts. Adopt the fanfic test as an explicit content-tier gate: fact-graph entities are either promoted to "story-grade" (unique, cross-referenced, quest-eligible) or remain texture, and only story-grade entities may appear in quests and named dialogue.
2. **Scale is a liability until meaning catches up.** (NMS, Elite Dangerous, file 11's Daggerfall.) Aegis's coarse overworld should be as small as the design tolerates, then grow across NG+ if wanted. Every site on the map should exist because the fact graph has something to say about it; empty distance is a cost the player pays, not content.
3. **Authorship in the parts, generation in the arrangement.** (Spelunky templates, Brogue machines, Qud grammars, WFC adjacency examples.) The human-authored layer in Aegis is the causal grammar's event templates, the storylet prose fragments, and the situation machines; the generator only selects, binds, and arranges. Budget authoring time accordingly: the templates are the game.
4. **Hard guarantees by construction beat validation by testing.** (Spelunky's carved solution path.) Generated quests, histories, and sites should be built around an invariant spine (target exists, path exists, causal chain is acyclic and complete) that the construction algorithm cannot violate, with the sim/journey/pilot autoplay tools as the second line of defense, not the first. This matches Aegis's existing engine-honest verification culture.
5. **Something with editorial authority must sit above the simulation.** (RimWorld's storyteller; Bethesda's pruning lesson in file 01.) The coarse tick generates true events; a pacing layer decides which reach the player, when, and how loudly, on a tension curve tied to the World Hostility Tier. Raw simulation output has terrible dramatic pacing in every game that ever shipped it.
6. **Players will learn the generator; plan the second hundred hours.** (Roguelike craft lore, Spelunky/Slay the Spire dailies.) Across infinite NG+, the same player sees many worlds. Freshness comes from growing the pools (event templates, factions archetypes, prose fragments) and, better, the *rules* (Dwarf Fortress's per-world myths): give each NG+ world a few generated world-scoped rules so worlds differ in kind.
7. **The generator version is part of the save format.** (Minecraft chunk borders.) Freeze the generator per world for that world's lifetime; apply generator upgrades only at NG+ crossings, where a fresh world is being born anyway. Decide this before the first save-format freeze, not after.
8. **Simulation on top of the world does more "no two playthroughs" work than generation of the world.** (Mount & Blade, Kenshi.) Worldgen buys freshness once, at world birth; the coarse tick buys it every session. Invest in faction dynamics, economy drift, and consequence persistence at least as heavily as in worldgen itself.
9. **Redemption is possible and always looks the same.** (NMS's arc.) If early Aegis worlds feel thin, the fix list is already known: authored spine content, stakes, player marks on the world, and things to build or govern. It is never "more variety in the generator."
10. **Evaluate the generator empirically, early.** ([Expressive range analysis](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228411962_Analyzing_the_expressive_range_of_a_level_generator), Elite's beauty parade.) Batch-generate hundreds of worlds in CI, compute distribution metrics over the fact graph (faction entanglement, war depth, relic linkage, name collision rates, quest-eligible entity counts per region), and heatmap them. Generators always cluster and always have holes; find them with tooling, not with players.

## Great patterns to borrow

- **Anchors + variation** (Qud's Joppa/Resheph, file 01). Already core to Aegis; this survey only adds that *cross-world* anchors (constants that survive NG+ crossings: a pantheon skeleton, a recurring order, an artifact lineage) buy back some of the fixed-world games' shared-reference value and give NG+ its sense of one continuing cosmos.
- **Generate-then-curate.** (Elite's beauty parade, 1984.) Run generation at dev time where a human can look at it. Concretely: a `world audit` CLI mode that renders a generated world's history as a readable chronicle for human review, plus curated "blessed seed" starting worlds for new players (hand-picked outputs of the same generator, so the first world every player sees passed a human eye, Spelunky-template style).
- **Rarity budgets.** (Folklore across loot-game design, visible in Brogue's restraint.) Rare things are stories only if they are actually rare. Give the generator hard per-world budgets: N legendary relics, M living legends, one world-scoped cataclysm. A generator asked for "some rare things" drifts toward many; a budget makes rarity a checkable invariant, and text makes each budgeted item cheap to make unique.
- **Names as attachment machinery.** (Dwarf Fortress, Qud, and the oldest folklore in the genre.) Named entities are what players retell; naming is where mad-libs are most visible. Invest in the name generator as a first-class subsystem: per-culture phonologies derived from the fact graph, names that carry history (patronymics, epithets earned from recorded events, places renamed after recorded battles), and collision auditing. In a TUI, names are the art assets.
- **Player-visible causality.** (Qud's relics referencing sultans; DF's Legends.) Every surfaced fact should be traceable in-fiction: the player can ask *why* (a historian, a book, a dialogue option) and get the actual causal chain from the graph. This is the single cheapest way to make generation feel authored, because authored worlds are exactly the ones where "why" has an answer. It also makes the fact graph self-verifying: a why-chain that dead-ends is a bug.
- **Marks on the world.** (Minecraft's real lesson.) The delta layer should record and re-surface player effects: cleared ruins stay cleared and get squatters, saved settlements erect something, death sites are marked, and NPCs reference all of it (the Nemesis memory layer from file 01 generalized to places). Across NG+ crossings, let a trace of the previous world's ending leak into the next world's generated history: the player becomes the fact graph's input, which is the strongest possible answer to "does my play matter in a generated world."
- **Density gradients.** (Valheim's distance-banded biomes; M&B's clustered map.) Sites dense with facts near settlements and roads, sparse wilderness between, danger and strangeness increasing along a legible gradient with in-fiction signposting (prices, refugees, rumors). The gradient is authored per-world by the generator's layout rules even though the map is random, keeping pacing designed while geography varies.
- **One fact, many surfaces.** (File 01's corroboration principle, promoted here to the primary anti-oatmeal weapon for text.) Every story-grade fact should be renderable through at least three surfaces (rumor, document, dialogue, price effect, epitaph) with different prose. Corroboration is what makes a generated fact feel *true*, and multiple renderings amortize the prose-authoring cost per fact.

## Things to avoid

- **The visible template, above all.** (Skyrim radiant per file 01, and every text mad-lib ever shipped.) In text this is the number-one existential risk. Never ship one sentence-skeleton per content type; never let two story-grade entities share a biography shape without at least one structural (not lexical) divergence; audit prose output across many seeds for repeated skeletons the way other games audit texture tiling.
- **Scale worship.** (NMS's 18 quintillion, Elite Dangerous's 400 billion, Daggerfall's Britain-sized map per file 11.) Never quote entity counts as a feature, internally or externally; the number is a promise the meaning layer must pay for. Every count in a design doc should be the *smallest* that supports the design.
- **Generation without consequence.** (Elite Dangerous's core failure: the generated 99.99% affects nothing.) If a generated entity can never alter a price, a quest, a dialogue, or a tick outcome, it should not exist in the fact graph. Aegis's derive-everything-from-the-graph architecture enforces this naturally; defend it against "flavor-only" content creep.
- **A landmark-free world.** (Wayfinding folklore from Minecraft/Valheim play: players navigate procedural worlds by remembered one-off features.) A coarse TUI overworld has no skyline, so landmarks must be *informational*: distinctive names, reputations, and map-legible uniqueness per region (the burned bridge, the singing ford). Budget at least one perceptually unique landmark per region and let travel text reference them as reference points.
- **Letting the generator touch the spine.** (File 01's central warning, reconfirmed by every game in this survey.) The main arc's *shape* stays authored; generation populates its cast and places from the graph but never invents its stakes.
- **Simulating what should be scripted, scripting what should be simulated.** (DF's decades-long simulation costs versus Qud's cheap grammar; Bethesda's "sounds great, plays terribly.") Aegis's history-first grammar is the right side of this line; the coarse tick should stay coarse. Any proposal to "just simulate it live" for narrative-bearing systems should be treated as a scope alarm.
- **Unbounded trust in validators.** (QA folklore: generated-content bugs cluster in interactions no validator anticipated.) Validators catch what they encode. Pair them with the expressive-range histograms (which find distributional weirdness no per-instance validator sees) and with autoplay soak testing through the existing sim/journey harness across hundreds of seeds per build.
- **Fixing thinness with variety.** (The NMS launch instinct, and the most common procgen failure reflex.) When playtests say a generated world feels thin, the reflex is more biomes, more name syllables, more templates. The survey's unanimous verdict: thinness is always a meaning deficit, never a variety deficit. The fix lives in stakes, consequence, corroboration, and authored spine, every time.
